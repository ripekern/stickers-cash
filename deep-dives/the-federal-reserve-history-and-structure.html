The Federal Reserve: History and Structure

A detailed look at how central banking power was centralized in the United States, from the Panic of 1907 to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.

This deep dive expands on the Federal Reserve section from "The ground under your feet." It covers the historical events that led to the creation of the Federal Reserve, the structure of the system, and the powers it holds. This is not a story about stability. It is a story about how monetary power was extracted from the people and concentrated in the hands of a few.

The push for a central bank

The Federal Reserve was born from a secret. In November 1910, six men — Senator Nelson Aldrich, Treasury official A. Piatt Andrew, and bankers Henry Davison, Frank Vanderlip, and Paul Warburg — met at the Jekyll Island Club off the coast of Georgia to write the plan for a new central bank. The meeting was kept secret for decades. They drafted the architecture of American monetary policy in private, on a private island, invited by nobody who was elected. The group included Aldrich, whose daughter would later marry John D. Rockefeller Jr. — connecting the plan directly to the banking and industrial interests that stood to benefit most.

The plan became the Aldrich Plan. It did not pass as written. Democrats, including Woodrow Wilson, opposed the Aldrich version of a central bank. What emerged was a compromise: a public Board of Governors in Washington, twelve regional Reserve Banks, and a new currency backed by the government. President Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913. The twelve Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914.

The Federal Reserve's own historians note that the technical details of the final bill "closely resembled" the Aldrich Plan. That is the origin story. A small group of bankers and officials drafted the architecture in private. The final law kept the core. It was marketed as a stabilizer, but what it delivered was a mechanism for concentrating monetary power — away from voters, away from the market, and into the hands of a small committee that meets behind closed doors.

What the Federal Reserve is

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States. It is not a normal bank. You cannot walk in and open a checking account. It is the bank for banks and for the federal government.

Its structure is a hybrid:

  • Board of Governors: seven governors in Washington, nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. They serve staggered 14-year terms.
  • Twelve regional Reserve Banks: operating arms in cities such as New York, Chicago, and St. Louis. Member banks hold stock in their district Reserve Bank, but the Fed says the system is not "owned" by anyone in the ordinary sense, and the stock does not carry normal control or profit rights.
  • Federal Open Market Committee: twelve officials—the seven governors plus five Reserve Bank presidents—who set monetary policy at least eight times a year.

The Fed describes itself as "independent within the government." Congress sets the goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed chooses the tools. Governors are appointed, not elected. Once appointed, they cannot be removed for their policy views. The people do not vote on the interest rate.

What it can do

This is where the power becomes concrete. The Fed can:

  • Set the price of credit. It sets a target for the federal funds rate, the rate banks pay to borrow reserves from each other. That rate ripples into mortgages, car loans, credit cards, and savings accounts. When the Fed raises rates, your mortgage payment goes up. When it lowers rates, your savings earn less. The Fed controls the price of money for everyone, and no one voted for it.
  • Create reserves. When the Fed buys Treasury or mortgage securities, it pays with newly created digital reserves—money that only banks can hold at the Fed. This is how quantitative easing works. It is a keystroke, not a printing press. Between 2008 and 2020, the Fed created $4 trillion in reserves. The people who owned stocks and real estate got richer. Everyone else got inflation.
  • Act as lender of last resort. In a panic, it can lend to banks against collateral through the discount window. In "unusual and exigent" circumstances, Section 13(3) lets it lend more broadly. In 2008, the Fed lent trillions to banks in secret. The recipients were not disclosed until years later, after lawsuits forced the Fed to release the records. The banks that got the loans were the same banks that had caused the crisis.
  • Supervise banks. It regulates bank holding companies and state-chartered member banks, and it helps set the rules that decide how much capital and liquidity banks must hold. The regulators are supposed to protect the public. In practice, they protect the banks. The same people who caused the 2008 crisis were bailed out by the same regulators who were supposed to prevent it.
  • Run the plumbing. It operates Fedwire and FedNow, clears checks, and settles payments for the financial system. Every digital dollar you send goes through the Fed's pipes. The Fed controls the infrastructure. The banks control the money. The people control nothing.
  • Issue currency. Federal Reserve notes are liabilities of the Fed. The Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing produces the paper, and the Reserve Banks put it into circulation. The paper in your wallet is not money. It is an IOU from the Fed, a promise to pay that can never be redeemed for anything of value.

The Fed can create money. It cannot create goods. It can add zeros to a ledger; it cannot add bread, oil, houses, or hours of labor. That distinction is the whole story. When the supply of claims grows faster than the supply of real things, the claims buy less. That is not inflation. That is theft. The Fed creates money out of nothing, and the people who hold dollars pay the price with their purchasing power.

The power shift

Before 1913, the United States had no permanent central bank. Credit was created by commercial banks. After 1913, a committee in Washington gained the power to expand reserves, set the price of credit, and decide which banks got liquidity.

That is what "central banking power taken from the people" means in practice. The bankers met in secret at Jekyll Island to design it. The structure they built concentrates power in the hands of appointed officials who do not answer to voters. The real decisions — who gets liquidity, which banks survive, how much money exists — are made behind closed doors. Bank ledgers are private. You cannot see where the money goes, who benefits, or what collateral backs the loans. Bitcoin is different: every transaction is public, every block is auditable, and the entire supply is verifiable by anyone. That is the accountability banks do not offer.

The private ledger

The Fed publishes its balance sheet. It publishes its meeting minutes. It publishes its financial statements. But the real decisions are made in private. Who gets the loans? What collateral backs them? Which banks are bailed out? You cannot see. The Fed's ledger is not transparent. It is a curated show. The totals are public. The details are hidden.

Contrast this with Bitcoin Cash. Every transaction is recorded on a public ledger. Every block is auditable. The entire supply is verifiable by anyone. No one can create new coins without the network's consent. No one can hide where the money goes. The ledger is open, transparent, and enforced by mathematics rather than by private agreements and regulatory capture.

The exit

The Federal Reserve is a private cartel that controls the money supply. It was created by bankers, for bankers, and it operates in their interests, not yours. The money you hold is a liability of a bank. It is a promise to pay. The bank can create new dollars whenever it wants. Your purchasing power is diluted every time the bank creates new deposits. You cannot see it happen. You cannot audit the bank's ledger. You cannot verify the collateral.

Bitcoin Cash is the exit. It is not a liability of a bank. It is not a promise to pay. It is a digital bearer asset that you control with a private key. No central bank can create new BCH. The supply is fixed at 21 million coins. Every transaction is recorded on a public ledger that anyone can audit. You do not need to trust a central bank. You do not need to accept their IOUs. You can hold money that is not someone else's liability.

The choice is between a private central bank that creates money in secret and a public ledger that creates money through transparent consensus. The Fed creates dollars on private ledgers that you cannot audit. Bitcoin creates coins on a public ledger that everyone can verify. The difference is accountability. With the Fed, you trust. With bitcoin, you verify.

The power grab

The Fed was marketed as a lender of last resort that could stop bank panics. What it actually delivered was a mechanism for a small committee to expand the money supply and set the price of credit for everyone — without ever facing an election. The "stability" it promised was the bait. The concentration of monetary power in the hands of unelected officials was the result.
