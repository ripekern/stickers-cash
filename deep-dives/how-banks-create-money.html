How Banks Create Money

A detailed look at how commercial banks create deposits when they make loans, with official documentation from central banks.

This deep dive expands on the money creation section from "Four leaks in every digital dollar." It covers the mechanics of how banks create deposits, with citations from central bank publications.

The process, step by step

Banks do not print paper dollars. They create deposits—the digital money in your account—when they make loans. The loan and the deposit are created together, on the bank's balance sheet.

What happensYouThe bank
You sign a $300,000 mortgage.You owe $300,000 plus interest.It records a $300,000 loan as an asset.
The bank credits your account.You have a $300,000 deposit.It records a $300,000 deposit as a liability.
You pay the seller.Your deposit moves.The loan stays on its books; the deposit moves to another bank.
You repay over time.You work and pay principal plus interest.The deposit is destroyed as the loan is repaid; the bank keeps the interest.

The bank did not wait for a saver to deposit $300,000. It created the deposit and the loan at the same time. That is not a metaphor. The Bank of England says it plainly:

"Whenever a bank makes a loan, it creates a corresponding deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money."

— Bank of England, "Money creation in the modern economy" (2014)

The European Central Bank says the same thing:

"In the modern economy, commercial banks are the creators of the majority of the money in circulation. […] Bank loans create deposits."

— European Central Bank, "The monetary policy of the ECB" (2011)

The Federal Reserve confirms it:

"Banks create money when they make loans. […] The act of lending creates new deposits."

— Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, "Modern Money Mechanics" (1992)

The reserves come after

This is the part that breaks the "banks are just intermediaries" story. After the bank creates the loan and the deposit, it may need reserves to meet regulatory requirements or to settle with other banks. But the reserves are not a prerequisite. They are a consequence.

The Bank of England explains:

"The reality of how money is created today differs from the description found in some economics textbooks: Rather than banks receiving deposits when households save and then lending them out, bank lending creates deposits."

The order is:

  1. The bank creates a loan and a deposit.
  2. The deposit circulates as money.
  3. If the bank needs reserves, it borrows them from other banks or from the central bank.
  4. The central bank supplies reserves on demand (at a price) to keep the system liquid.

The central bank does not "print money" that the banks then lend. The banks create deposits first. The central bank provides reserves afterward to keep the system stable.

Why this matters

If banks create deposits when they lend, then the money supply is not fixed. It expands when banks lend and contracts when loans are repaid. The central bank can influence this by setting interest rates and reserve requirements, but it does not control it directly.

This is why the money supply has grown from about $1 trillion in 1980 to $21.7 trillion in 2024. It is not the government "printing money." It is the banking system creating deposits through lending. Each new loan adds to the money supply. Each repayment removes it.

The implication is significant: the money you hold is a claim on the banking system, not a claim on a fixed pool of real resources. The banking system can create more claims than there are real resources to back them. That is how inflation happens—not because the government prints paper, but because the banking system creates deposits faster than the economy produces goods and services.

The key point

Money is not a thing that exists independently of the banking system. It is a claim that the banking system creates when it lends. The central bank can influence the process, but it does not control it. The banking system creates money, and the rest of us live with the consequences.

Sources for this page: Bank of England, "Money creation in the modern economy" (2014); European Central Bank, "The monetary policy of the ECB" (2011); Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, "Modern Money Mechanics" (1992).
