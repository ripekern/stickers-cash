When the Dollar Became Digital

The dollar did not become digital on one day. It became digital one layer at a time, from the Federal Reserve in 1913 to FedNow in 2023.

The transition from paper to pixels was not a single event. It was a series of legal and technical changes that turned the dollar from a physical thing you held into a ledger entry you trusted.

  1. 1913 — a central bank. The Federal Reserve Act created the Fed and a new national currency system. The Fed could create reserves and lend in a panic.
  2. 1933–34 — the domestic gold link breaks. In the Depression, the government stopped Americans from redeeming dollars for gold and then devalued the dollar against gold. The dollar was still linked internationally, but at home it was becoming a managed claim.
  3. 1944 — Bretton Woods. The postwar system tied other currencies to the dollar, and the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce. The dollar became the world's reserve currency.
  4. 1971 — the gold window closes. President Nixon suspended the conversion of dollars into gold. The Fed's own history says the international monetary system "turned into a fiat one." From then on, the dollar's supply was backed by the government's authority and the Fed's management, not by a fixed weight of metal.
  5. 2008 and 2020 — digital reserves at scale. During the financial crisis and the pandemic, the Fed bought trillions in bonds and paid with newly created reserves. The balance sheet went from about $0.9 trillion in 2007 to $6.76 trillion in October 2026.
  6. 2023 — instant settlement. The Fed launched FedNow, a real-time payment system. It made bank money faster. It did not make it cash.

Today the Fed itself says Americans "have long held money predominantly in digital form—for example in bank accounts, payment apps or through online transactions." Its 2022 payments paper puts it more plainly: "Commercial bank money is the digital form of money that is most commonly used by the public."

That is the system you use every day. Your paycheck is a deposit. Your Venmo balance is a deposit or a claim on one. Your debit card is an instruction to move a deposit. The paper bills in your wallet are a small minority of the money supply. The rest is a stack of IOUs on screens.

What changed when money became digital

Paper cash is final. When you hand someone a twenty-dollar bill, the transaction is done. No bank can reverse it. No company can freeze it. No government can track it after the fact. Cash is also private. The bill has no name on it. The other person does not see your balance or your history.

Digital dollars are different. They are watched, freezable, and reversible. Every transaction is recorded in a bank's database. The bank can freeze your account if something looks suspicious. The government can subpoena the records. The company processing the payment takes a cut.

These are not bugs. They are features of a system designed for control and convenience. But they are also the source of the four leaks: watched, freezable, fee-taxed, and melting.

The key insight

The dollar did not become digital overnight. It was a gradual process of turning physical cash into ledger entries. Each step made money more convenient but less like cash. By the time you got here, the paper in your wallet was already a minority of the money supply. The rest is a stack of promises backed by rules and institutions.
