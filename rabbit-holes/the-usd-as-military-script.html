The USD as Military Script

What is military script, and why do some argue Federal Reserve Notes are actually military currency? The history, the legal claims, and what courts have said.

This rabbit hole explores a controversial claim: that Federal Reserve Notes are not ordinary currency but rather a form of military script intended only for government use. It's a theory that comes up in various forms, often in discussions about the nature of money and the limits of state power.

What is military script?

Military script (sometimes spelled "scrip") is currency issued by a military authority for use in specific contexts, typically occupied territories or military installations. It is not legal tender in the ordinary sense. It exists because the issuing authority needs a way to pay troops, purchase supplies, or manage an occupied economy without using the sovereign currency of the territory.

Historical examples include:

  • Allied Military Marks — issued by Allied forces in occupied Germany after World War II. These were not legal tender in Germany proper; they existed to pay occupying forces and manage the occupation economy.
  • Allied Military Lire — used in occupied Italy during and after WWII.
  • Japanese invasion money — issued by Japan in occupied territories during WWII (e.g., "banana money" in Southeast Asia).
  • U.S. military payment certificates — used to pay U.S. troops stationed abroad, particularly in places like Vietnam and South Korea, to prevent black market activity and currency manipulation.

The key characteristic of military script is that it is not intended for general circulation among the civilian population of the issuing country. It is a tool of military administration.

The claim about Federal Reserve Notes

Some theorists argue that Federal Reserve Notes share characteristics with military script. The argument goes roughly like this:

  • Federal Reserve Notes are not "dollars" in the constitutional sense. The Constitution (Article I, Section 10) prohibits states from making anything but gold and silver coin a tender in payment of debts, but it does not prohibit the federal government from issuing paper currency.
  • The Federal Reserve is a hybrid entity — part government, part private banking corporation. Its notes are technically liabilities of the Federal Reserve, not of the U.S. Treasury.
  • Military script has historically been issued by authorities operating under emergency or occupation conditions. Some argue that the Federal Reserve was created during a period of financial instability (the Panic of 1907) and operates under similar emergency assumptions.
  • The claim is that Federal Reserve Notes were originally intended only for government transactions (paying soldiers, government contractors, etc.) and were never meant to be the general currency of the American people.

This is a historical and legal discussion about the nature of Federal Reserve Notes. While the theory that they are military script has not been accepted by courts, understanding the argument helps clarify what money is and how it functions in a modern economy.

What the law actually says

Federal Reserve Notes are legal tender for all debts, public and private. This is established by statute (31 U.S.C. § 5103): "United States coins and currency (including Federal Reserve notes and circulating notes of Federal Reserve banks and national banks) are legal tender for all debts, public charges, taxes, and dues."

Courts have consistently held that:

  • Federal Reserve Notes are valid currency and must be accepted for payment of debts.
  • The Federal Reserve is a legitimate government-created entity, not a private corporation operating outside constitutional constraints.
  • Individuals cannot refuse to accept Federal Reserve Notes on the grounds that they are "not real money" or "military script."

The legal tender status of Federal Reserve Notes has been upheld repeatedly, including by the Supreme Court. The theory that they are military script has never been accepted by any court.

Why this matters for understanding money

Even though the "military script" theory has no legal standing, it raises interesting questions about the nature of money:

  • What gives money value? Federal Reserve Notes have value because people accept them and because the government requires taxes to be paid in them. They are not backed by gold or any physical commodity.
  • Who controls the money supply? The Federal Reserve, as we've discussed, has the power to create money. This is a significant concentration of power, whether or not the notes are "military script."
  • What are the alternatives? The existence of Bitcoin Cash and other decentralized currencies shows that money does not require a central authority. The question is not whether Federal Reserve Notes are "military script" but whether a decentralized alternative is preferable.

The deeper issue

The real issue is not whether Federal Reserve Notes are technically military script (they are not, according to law and courts). The issue is that the monetary system is centralized, controlled by a small group of unelected officials, and subject to political influence.

Whether you call it military script, fiat currency, or legal tender, the fact remains: the money you use is created by an authority you do not control, and its value depends on trust in that authority. Bitcoin Cash offers an alternative: money that is created by a transparent, predictable process and controlled by the people who hold it.

The "military script" theory raises interesting questions about the nature of money and the relationship between citizens and the monetary system. Whether or not one finds the theory persuasive, it highlights the importance of understanding how money works and what alternatives exist.
