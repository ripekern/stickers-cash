What the Codes Actually Say

The distinction between codes and law. Why the United States Code applies to government officers, not private citizens.

Federal Reserve Notes are designated as legal tender under 31 U.S.C. § 5103. But this is a code, not a law. The United States Code (U.S.C.) is a codification of federal statutes that, by its own structure, applies to officers and agents of the federal government — not to private citizens in their private dealings.

Codes vs. Law

Codes and regulations are administrative instruments. They are not law in the sense of binding private individuals who have not consented to them. The legal tender provision tells us what the government will accept for debts owed to it — not what a private person must accept in private commerce.

Regulations promulgated under those codes carry the same limitation: they govern the conduct of government, not the rights of the people. When you read "shall" in the U.S.C., it typically means a government officer shall do something — not that you, as a private citizen, are compelled to comply.

Who Does the Code Apply To?

The U.S.C. is organized by subject matter, but its applicability depends on capacity. If you are acting in a private capacity — conducting your own business, using your own property, engaging in private commerce — the codes do not apply to you. They apply to:

  • Officers and employees of the United States
  • Agencies and instrumentalities of the federal government
  • Persons acting under color of federal authority
  • Those who have consented to the jurisdiction (through contracts, licenses, or benefits)

The legal tender statute (31 U.S.C. § 5103) tells federal officers what they must accept for government debts. It does not compel private merchants, private individuals, or private businesses to accept Federal Reserve Notes. A private restaurant can refuse cash. A private business can require credit cards. A private party can demand gold, silver, or Bitcoin Cash. The code does not reach into private commerce.

The Presumption of Applicability

The system operates on a presumption: if you are using Federal Reserve Notes, you must be a government officer or agent, because why else would you be using government currency? This presumption is not stated in the code — it is assumed by the system. The burden falls on you to rebut this presumption, which is difficult when the alternative currency has been systematically suppressed or marginalized.

This is where the military script argument becomes practical. If Federal Reserve Notes are government script, and you are using them exclusively, the system treats you as operating within the government's jurisdiction. The codes apply to you because you are presumed to be part of the government's operations.

Rebutting the Presumption

The presumption can be rebutted by demonstrating that you are acting in a private capacity and have not consented to the jurisdiction. Using alternative currency, declaring your status as a private individual, and avoiding government benefits and licenses are ways to rebut the presumption. But the system is designed to make this difficult — tax codes, reporting requirements, and regulatory frameworks all push people toward using Federal Reserve Notes and accepting government jurisdiction.

This leads to the next question: if you are using government script, are you actually a government officer?
