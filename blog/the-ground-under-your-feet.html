The ground under your feet

Cash is final, private, and needs no permission. Then a central bank, a gold window, and a digital rail replaced it — and put every dollar on thin ice.

Post 1 of 30 · 8 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Right now, without realizing it, you are walking on thin ice.

Every paycheck that goes into a bank, every payment through Venmo, Cash App, Apple Pay, or a credit card feels safe because it has always worked. But look down: there is nothing solid under you. You don't actually control that money. A middleman does.

That middleman is not one villain. It is a system: a central bank, commercial banks, payment processors, and the apps on your phone. The system was built over a century. Once you see the layers, the ice makes sense.

The short version

  • Cash works because it is final, private, and needs no permission. It also can't go through a screen.
  • Digital dollars are watched, freezable, fee-taxed, and diluted. The price level, not just the inflation rate, sits at a record high.
  • The Federal Reserve is the central bank created in 1913. It can create reserves, set the price of credit, and act as lender of last resort. It is independent within government.
  • Bitcoin Cash is peer-to-peer internet cash with a fixed supply. You hold it yourself and can spend any fraction of it in seconds for under a cent.

And middlemen can — and do — track it, take a cut, freeze it, reverse it, leak it, and devalue it over time.

Think about cash for a second. That's solid ground.

When you hand a $10 bill to a neighbor, a farmer, or a kid mowing your lawn, it's done. No permission needed. No 3% to Visa. No bank holding it for "3–5 business days." No company recording what you bought, where you were, and who you were with.

Cash is anonymous without middlemen. The bill doesn't have your name on it. The other person doesn't see your balance or your history. That's not criminal — that's normal life. It's buying a gift without being tracked, donating without getting spammed forever, tipping without a fee, surviving without needing ID, a password, and approval.

That's why people have always needed cash: privacy, freedom, access for everyone, and final payment with no extra fee.

But here's the danger: you can't use cash on the internet.

You can't push a dollar bill through a screen to pay someone in another city, get paid for remote work, buy online, help family overseas, or donate to someone in trouble. Online, cash doesn't exist. So you were forced onto the thin ice — 100% dependent on banks and apps for modern life.

The ground was not always ice

For most of history, money was a thing you held. A gold coin, a silver dollar, a banknote issued by a local bank. You could lose it, but no one could freeze it with a click. There was no permanent central bank setting the price of credit for the whole country.

Then the United States created one. Not all at once, and not by accident. The change came after a panic, a secret meeting, and a law signed in December 1913. Once you see how it happened, the ice under your feet stops looking like a natural fact. It starts looking like a design.

1907: the panic that changed the rules

In 1907, depositors lost confidence in New York trust companies. Crowds lined up to withdraw cash. Banks suspended payments. J.P. Morgan, the most powerful banker of the era, organized a private rescue. The panic exposed a structural problem: the United States had no permanent lender of last resort that could create liquidity when the system froze.

The official history is not a secret. The Federal Reserve's own historians describe the Panic of 1907 as the catalyst. Congress created the National Monetary Commission. Its chairman, Senator Nelson Aldrich, wanted a central bank that could supply an "elastic" currency. In November 1910, six men met at the Jekyll Island Club off the coast of Georgia to write the plan. The meeting was kept secret for decades. The group included Aldrich, Treasury official A. Piatt Andrew, and bankers Henry Davison, Frank Vanderlip, and Paul Warburg.

The plan became the Aldrich Plan. It did not pass as written. Democrats, including Woodrow Wilson, opposed the Aldrich version of a central bank. What emerged was a compromise: a public Board of Governors in Washington, twelve regional Reserve Banks, and a new currency backed by the government. President Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act on December 23, 1913. The twelve Reserve Banks opened on November 16, 1914.

The Federal Reserve's own historians note that the technical details of the final bill "closely resembled" the Aldrich Plan. That is the origin story. A small group of bankers and officials drafted the architecture in private. The final law kept the core. Supporters sold it as stability. The structure also concentrated power.

What the Federal Reserve is

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States. It is not a normal bank. You cannot walk in and open a checking account. It is the bank for banks and for the federal government.

Its structure is a hybrid:

  • Board of Governors: seven governors in Washington, nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. They serve staggered 14-year terms.
  • Twelve regional Reserve Banks: operating arms in cities such as New York, Chicago, and St. Louis. Member banks hold stock in their district Reserve Bank, but the Fed says the system is not "owned" by anyone in the ordinary sense, and the stock does not carry normal control or profit rights.
  • Federal Open Market Committee: twelve officials—the seven governors plus five Reserve Bank presidents—who set monetary policy at least eight times a year.

The Fed describes itself as "independent within the government." Congress sets the goals: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The Fed chooses the tools. Governors are appointed, not elected. Once appointed, they cannot be removed for their policy views. The people do not vote on the interest rate.

What it can do

This is where the power becomes concrete. The Fed can:

  • Set the price of credit. It sets a target for the federal funds rate, the rate banks pay to borrow reserves from each other. That rate ripples into mortgages, car loans, credit cards, and savings accounts.
  • Create reserves. When the Fed buys Treasury or mortgage securities, it pays with newly created digital reserves—money that only banks can hold at the Fed. This is how quantitative easing works. It is a keystroke, not a printing press.
  • Act as lender of last resort. In a panic, it can lend to banks against collateral through the discount window. In "unusual and exigent" circumstances, Section 13(3) lets it lend more broadly.
  • Supervise banks. It regulates bank holding companies and state-chartered member banks, and it helps set the rules that decide how much capital and liquidity banks must hold.
  • Run the plumbing. It operates Fedwire and FedNow, clears checks, and settles payments for the financial system.
  • Issue currency. Federal Reserve notes are liabilities of the Fed. The Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing produces the paper, and the Reserve Banks put it into circulation.

The Fed can create money. It cannot create goods. It can add zeros to a ledger; it cannot add bread, oil, houses, or hours of labor. That distinction is the whole story. When the supply of claims grows faster than the supply of real things, the claims buy less. The inflation chapter later shows the arithmetic.

The power shift

Before 1913, the United States had no permanent central bank. Credit was created by commercial banks, and panics were handled by private financiers or by suspending payments. After 1913, a committee in Washington could expand reserves, set the price of credit, and decide which banks got liquidity in a crisis.

That is what "central banking power taken from the people" means in practice. It does not require a secret room or a shadowy family. It is a legal structure. Congress created the Fed, and Congress can change it. But the daily decisions about how much money exists are made by appointed officials, not by voters or by the market.

The Fed is not a secret cabal. It publishes its balance sheet, its meeting minutes, and its financial statements. You can read them. But transparency is not the same as accountability. You can watch the decision, and you still do not get a vote.

The trade

The Fed was sold as a stabilizer: a lender of last resort that could stop bank panics. It is also a centralizer: a small committee can expand the supply of money and set the price of credit for everyone. Both things are true. The question is whether the stability is worth the concentration of power.
