The fee that is almost nothing

BCH fees are not a percentage of your payment. They are a price for space in a block — and BCH has a lot of space.

Post 14 of 30 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

The first time you see a BCH fee, it can look like a mistake. A payment worth hundreds of dollars might cost a fraction of a cent. A payment worth one dollar might cost the same. The fee is not calculated as a percentage. It is a price for space.

You pay for bytes, not dollars

A transaction takes up space in a block. The size depends on how many inputs and outputs it has, not on how much value it moves. A simple payment with one input and two outputs is small. A transaction that sweeps hundreds of tiny inputs is larger, but still cheap by the standards of global settlement.

That is why the same fee can cover a one-dollar payment and a thousand-dollar payment. The network is not taxing the value. It is allocating room in the next block, and BCH makes that room plentiful.

Why BCH fees stay low

BCH has much more block space than Bitcoin. It started with 8 MB blocks in 2017, moved to 32 MB in 2018, and since 2024 has used an adaptive block-size limit that can grow with demand while keeping 32 MB as a floor. More capacity means less bidding, even when the network is busy.

In normal conditions, a simple BCH transaction costs a fraction of a cent. That is not a promotional rate or a temporary subsidy. It is the result of a design that expands the pipe as demand arrives. The fee stays low because the network keeps making room.

A fee is rent, not a tax

The fee does not scale with the amount you send. It buys block space. If the network is quiet, that space is cheap. If the network is busy, BCH's adaptive capacity keeps the queue moving. The protocol does not care whether you are buying a sticker or a server; it cares about the size of the transaction.

What the fee pays for

Miners do two jobs: they order transactions into blocks and secure the chain with proof of work. They are paid through the block subsidy plus transaction fees. BCH's design makes payments cheap enough that people use the chain, and that usage creates a healthy fee stream alongside the subsidy. It is a volume model: cheap payments, real usage, and a network that stays secure.

That is a different approach from a chain that rations space and charges high fees. BCH chooses utility. The result is a settlement layer that works for a four-dollar sticker and a four-thousand-dollar order alike.

The whole cost of a payment

The network fee is tiny. The rest of commerce — exchange spreads, taxes, shipping, and accounting — is normal business, not a BCH fee. On-chain, the cost of moving value is predictable and low. That is the part BCH controls, and it controls it well.

So when someone says “BCH fees are almost nothing,” the precise claim is about the protocol layer. The network fee for a simple transaction is usually tiny, and the design keeps it that way as usage grows. A payment system that can move a dollar without a thirty-cent toll is a different kind of system.
