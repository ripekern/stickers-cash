Scaling without a ceiling

BCH scales by expanding the pipe. The Adaptive Block Limit Algorithm lets capacity follow demand instead of freezing it at a fixed number.

Post 19 of 30 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Most payment networks scale by making the queue expensive. When demand outruns capacity, users bid up fees until some of them give up. Bitcoin Cash takes a different route: it makes the pipe bigger.

Capacity is the answer

A blockchain confirms transactions in batches called blocks. BCH's design goal is to keep blocks clear of a backlog, so a payment fits in the next block without a bidding war. A coffee buyer should not compete with a bank settlement for space. A merchant should not have to explain that the payment will arrive eventually.

That goal is why BCH's block size is not fixed at 1 MB. It launched in 2017 with 8 MB blocks, moved to 32 MB in 2018, and then stopped treating the number as a permanent political settlement.

32 MB was a floor, not a finish line

In May 2024, BCH activated the Adaptive Block Limit Algorithm, or ABLA. The 32 MB limit remains as a floor. When demand rises, the limit can rise with it. When demand falls, the network keeps the floor and stays ready for the next burst.

The important change is not the exact number. It is the governance. Before ABLA, every increase meant another upgrade and another public argument. After ABLA, capacity follows usage automatically. The network stops asking, “Is it time to raise the number?” and starts asking, “How much traffic is arriving?” That is a more boring question, which is exactly what money infrastructure needs.

How the limit follows demand

ABLA watches how full recent blocks are. When blocks are consistently near the limit, it raises the limit. When there is plenty of spare room, it stays near the floor. The adjustment is gradual, so node operators, miners, wallets, and merchants get a smooth transition instead of a sudden jump.

The 32 MB floor remains underneath, and capacity grows in step with demand. The result is a network that can keep clearing the queue as usage grows, without turning every payment into an auction.

What clearing the queue changes

When blocks clear the pending transactions, several things happen at once. Fees stay low and predictable. Transactions confirm in the next block instead of waiting for a gap. Zero-conf acceptance is fast and practical because the queue is clear. Small payments stay viable.

The low fee is not a subsidy. It is the result of capacity outrunning demand. That is why a BCH transaction can cost a fraction of a cent while a congested network can charge dollars for the same amount of block space. The difference is architecture.

It also changes what builders can assume. A merchant can price a four-dollar item without building a thirty-cent payment toll into it. A game can send a tiny payment. A community can tip a creator without the tip being eaten by the rail. Those use cases work on day one.

Built to grow

BCH's capacity model is simple: keep the floor high, let the limit follow demand, and keep validation costs manageable as the network grows. That is how a payment network scales without turning every transaction into an auction. The 2018 and 2021 stress tests showed the design working under real load. The next post is the evidence.
