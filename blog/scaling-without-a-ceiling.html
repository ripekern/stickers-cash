Scaling without a ceiling

BCH does not manage congestion with high fees. It expands the pipe. The Adaptive Block Limit Algorithm lets capacity follow demand instead of freezing it at a fixed number.

Post 19 of 20 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Most payment networks scale by making the queue expensive. When demand outruns capacity, users bid up fees until some of them give up. The queue clears, but only because the price pushed people out. Bitcoin Cash takes a different route: it makes the pipe bigger.

The queue is the problem

A blockchain confirms transactions in batches called blocks. If the block limit is small, demand can outrun space. Transactions wait. Fees rise. Confirmation times become a lottery. For a payment network, that is not a minor inconvenience. It is the difference between money that works and money that only works when the network is quiet.

BCH's design goal is to clear the queue. At normal demand, a transaction should fit in the next block without a bidding war. A coffee buyer should not be competing with a bank settlement for space. A merchant should not have to explain that the payment will arrive eventually.

That goal is why BCH's block size is not a fixed 1 MB. It launched in 2017 with 8 MB blocks, moved to 32 MB in 2018, and then stopped treating the number as a permanent political settlement.

32 MB was a floor, not a finish line

In May 2024, BCH activated the Adaptive Block Limit Algorithm, or ABLA. The 32 MB limit remains as a floor. When demand rises, the limit can rise with it. When demand falls, the network does not need to shrink the pipe just to make a point.

The important change is not the exact number. It is the governance. Before ABLA, every increase meant another argument, another upgrade, another chance for the community to split over a parameter. After ABLA, capacity can follow usage without a recurring block-size war. The network stops asking, “Is it time to raise the number?” and starts asking, “How much traffic is actually arriving?”

That is a more boring question, which is exactly what money infrastructure needs.

How the limit follows demand

ABLA watches how full recent blocks are. When blocks are consistently near the limit, it raises the limit. When there is plenty of spare room, it stays near the floor. The adjustment is gradual, so node operators, miners, wallets, and merchants are not asked to absorb a sudden jump overnight.

This does not mean BCH has infinite capacity. Every block still has to propagate, be validated, and be stored. Bigger blocks require more bandwidth and disk space. The adaptive limit is a way to grow capacity in step with demand while keeping those costs visible and manageable, not a claim that physics has been repealed.

What it does mean is that BCH is not designed around permanent scarcity. The limit is a moving ceiling, not a fixed wall.

What clearing the queue changes

When blocks clear the pending transactions, several things happen at once. Fees stay low and predictable. Transactions confirm in the next block instead of waiting for a gap. Zero-conf acceptance becomes safer because there is less incentive to replace a payment with a higher bid. Small payments stay viable.

The low fee is not a subsidy. It is the result of capacity outrunning demand. That is why a BCH transaction can cost a fraction of a cent while a congested network can charge dollars for the same amount of block space. The difference is not generosity. It is architecture.

It also changes what builders can assume. A merchant can price a four-dollar item without building a thirty-cent payment toll into it. A game can send a tiny payment. A community can tip a creator without the tip being eaten by the rail. Those use cases do not require a separate layer to make the economics work on day one.

The trade-off is real

Bigger blocks are not free. They require more bandwidth, more storage, and more validation work. If capacity grows faster than the ability of ordinary operators to keep up, the network becomes more dependent on large infrastructure. That is the centralization risk, and it is legitimate.

BCH's answer is not to deny the trade-off. It is to grow the limit gradually, keep the floor at 32 MB, and let the network demonstrate that the resource cost is manageable. The bet is that cheap, final payments are worth the extra capacity, and that the network can scale without turning every transaction into an auction.

That bet has been tested. The next post is about what happened when people deliberately tried to break it.
