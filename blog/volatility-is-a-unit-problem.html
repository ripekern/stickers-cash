Volatility is a unit problem

BCH looks volatile in dollars. Dollars look volatile in BCH. Which one is moving — and which ruler are you using?

Post 17 of 30 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Open a BCH price chart and you will see a jagged line. Open a dollar chart and you will see a line that looks stable — but only because the dollar is the ruler. Change the ruler, and the picture changes. Volatility is partly a property of the asset and partly a property of the unit you measure it in.

Two monies, one exchange rate

The price of BCH in dollars is an exchange rate between two monies. When the number moves, it does not automatically mean BCH became less useful. It means the market's relative valuation changed. The dollar can be losing purchasing power at the same time BCH is losing dollar price. Both can be true.

This is why “BCH is volatile” and “the dollar is volatile” can both be said honestly. The dollar is not volatile against itself. It is volatile against goods, services, and other monies over time. We are just used to measuring everything in the unit that is slowly changing.

The unit-of-account problem

Money does several jobs: a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. BCH is designed to be good at exchange and scarce as a store of value. It is not yet a common unit of account. Most people still price their rent, groceries, and salary in local currency. That makes BCH feel like an asset that swings, because every wallet shows a dollar value next to the coin balance.

If you measure your stack in BCH, the stack does not change when the dollar price moves. Ten BCH is still ten BCH. That is a useful psychological anchor, but it is not the whole story. What those ten coins can buy is the real question. A fixed supply does not guarantee a stable short-term price. It guarantees a fixed supply.

A fixed supply is not a fixed price

Scarcity is a long-term property. Price is a short-term auction. Demand, liquidity, speculation, regulation, and news can move the auction far more than the supply schedule moves in a year.

Why the swings are so large

BCH trades in a global market that is still small compared with established financial markets. Liquidity is thinner. A large order can move the price. News and sentiment can move it faster. The market is also open all the time, which means there is no closing bell to stop a bad day.

As a market deepens, volatility often falls, but there is no guarantee and no schedule. Adoption can reduce it. Speculation can increase it. Regulation can change it overnight. A holder who needs certainty next month is not wrong to notice that.

Separate the spending money

Volatility is easier to live with when the money you need soon is not the money that swings. A phone wallet with a small spendable balance can cover everyday purchases while a longer-term holding sits elsewhere. That is not a prediction about price. It is a way to avoid being forced to sell at a bad moment because the rent is due.

The same principle applies to merchants. A shop can price in local currency and convert at the moment of sale, or keep some BCH and accept the price risk. Neither choice is magic. One reduces short-term volatility; the other keeps more exposure to the asset.

“Think in coins, not dollars” is a useful correction to price obsession, but it is not a license to ignore purchasing power. The point of money is what it can buy. A coin mindset should make you ask better questions, not fewer.

BCH has a fixed supply and a volatile market. Those two facts are not contradictory. One is a protocol rule. The other is a market still discovering how large and useful the network will become. You can hold both facts at once without pretending either one is the whole truth.
