Self-custody without the fear

Self-custody is not a test of bravery. It is a backup plan, a threat model, and a few habits repeated until they are boring.

Post 16 of 20 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Self-custody has a reputation problem. It sounds like something for developers, hackers, and people who enjoy checking checksums. In practice, it is a set of simple habits. The hard part is not the software. The hard part is accepting that the safety net is gone.

That fear is rational. A bank can reverse a mistaken transfer. A BCH transaction cannot. A bank can reset a password. A wallet cannot. The answer is not to pretend the risk is zero. The answer is to reduce the ways you can lose the key and to practice before the amount matters.

Start with a practice wallet

Create a wallet with a small amount of BCH. Write down the seed phrase. Send a payment to yourself. Restore the wallet on a second device. Delete the app and restore it again. Do this while the amount is small enough that a mistake is annoying rather than life-changing.

The first goal is not maximum security. The first goal is familiarity. You want to know what the seed does, what happens when you restore, and what a transaction looks like before you are relying on it for rent.

Back up the seed properly

The seed phrase is the master backup. Write it on paper or stamp it into metal. Keep it offline. Do not photograph it, email it, save it in a password manager, or store it in cloud notes. Those are all convenient ways to create a second copy that a thief can reach.

Keep more than one copy in more than one place, so a fire or flood does not erase your money. If you use an optional passphrase, back it up separately from the seed. A passphrase can add security, but it can also create a wallet you cannot restore because you forgot which variation you used.

The backup test

A backup you have never restored is a hope. Test the restore on a spare device before the wallet holds anything important. If the balance comes back, the backup works. If it does not, you found out at the right time.

Know your threat model

Most losses are not cinematic hacks. They are lost seeds, forgotten passphrases, phishing sites, malware that swaps a copied address, and people who stored the seed in a place they thought was clever. The threat model matters:

  • Loss: fire, flood, moving, a dead phone, a forgotten backup. Use durable copies in separate locations.
  • Theft: malware, fake wallets, fake support, clipboard hijacks. Keep large amounts offline and verify addresses on the device.
  • Coercion: someone demands your keys. A smaller “spending wallet” can reduce what is exposed, but no technical setup solves every physical threat.
  • Inheritance: your family cannot recover what they cannot find. Leave instructions and a secure location, not a seed phrase in a public will.

Do not over-engineer the first wallet

Multisignature, hardware wallets, and complex inheritance plans are useful tools. They are also easy to misconfigure. A simple phone wallet with a tested seed backup is more secure than an elaborate setup you do not understand. Add complexity only when the amount and the threat justify it.

For larger savings, a hardware wallet that keeps the key offline is a reasonable step. For everyday spending, a phone wallet with a small balance is fine. The important boundary is between spending money and savings, not between “beginner” and “expert.”

Scams target the human, not the chain

No one needs your seed phrase to send you BCH. No wallet upgrade requires it by email. No giveaway is improved by it. Fake support accounts, fake apps, and fake “validation” pages all want the same thing. The protocol is not the weak point in those attacks. The person holding the key is.

The goal is not perfect security. The goal is fewer single points of failure. A tested backup, a separate spending wallet, and a healthy refusal to share the seed will prevent more losses than any advanced feature you do not need yet.
