A wallet is not an account

Your BCH does not sit inside an app. It sits on a public ledger. The wallet holds the one thing that can move it — and that changes who is really in charge.

Post 11 of 20 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Your bank account trains you to think about money in a very specific way. You log in. You see a balance. You ask the bank to move some of it. The bank checks your identity, checks the balance on its own ledger, and changes a number. The cash you think you own is really a promise recorded inside an institution.

A Bitcoin Cash wallet works differently. It does not hold coins the way a pocket holds bills. The coins are entries on a public ledger. The wallet holds the private keys that can prove ownership and authorize a transfer. The wallet is closer to a keychain than a vault.

What is actually in your wallet

A non-custodial wallet stores a secret and uses it to sign transactions. From that secret it derives public addresses you can share. When someone pays you, the payment is recorded on the blockchain as an output locked to one of your addresses. Your wallet scans the chain, finds outputs it can spend, and shows you a balance.

That is why nobody can reset your password. There is no account department. If you lose the secret, the wallet cannot ask a support agent to restore it. If someone else gets the secret, they can spend the money. The secret is the money.

Most modern wallets hide this behind a seed phrase: twelve or twenty-four words that can regenerate every key in the wallet. The words are not a password. They are the master backup. Anyone who has them has your wallet.

The one rule

If someone asks for your seed phrase, they are not helping you. No legitimate support agent, wallet update, giveaway, or exchange needs it. Type it only into a wallet you chose, on a device you control, and preferably not even then unless you are restoring.

Custodial is not the same as self-custodial

When you buy BCH on an exchange and leave it there, you do not hold BCH. You hold a claim on the exchange. That can be convenient. It is also the old model again: an institution records your balance, can freeze it, can lose it, and can change the terms. “Not your keys, not your coins” is a blunt slogan because the difference is blunt.

Self-custody means you control the key. It also means you control the mistakes. That is the trade: no permission, no intermediary, no undo button.

Hot wallets and cold wallets

A hot wallet is connected to the internet, usually on a phone or laptop. It is useful for spending money. A cold wallet keeps the key offline, often on a hardware device. It is useful for savings you do not touch often. The point is not to buy the most complicated setup. The point is to match the tool to the amount and the risk.

A practical first setup is simple: a phone wallet with a small spendable balance, a written seed backup stored somewhere safe, and a plan for anything larger. You do not need to understand every derivation path on day one. You do need to understand that the wallet is software, the key is the money, and the backup is the difference between a mistake and a disaster.

Before you put real savings into a new wallet, test the restore. Send a small amount. Close the wallet, restore it from the seed, and confirm the balance comes back. A backup you have never tested is a hope, not a backup.
