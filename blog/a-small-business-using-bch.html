A small business using BCH for international trade

A Vietnamese coffee exporter sells to a US roaster. The payment settles in minutes, costs pennies, and no one needs a bank account or approval.

Post 34 of 37 · 5 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Linh runs a small coffee export business in Da Lat, Vietnam. She sources beans from local farmers and sells to specialty roasters in the US, Europe, and Japan. For years, she used bank wires and letters of credit to handle payments.

Then she discovered Bitcoin Cash. Now she accepts BCH payments from about 20% of her customers. The payments settle in minutes, cost pennies, and bypass the banking system entirely.

The old way

International trade traditionally relies on bank wires, letters of credit, and trade finance. For a $10,000 shipment of coffee, the payment process looks like this:

  • The buyer's bank sends a wire to the seller's bank. The wire takes three to five business days and costs $25 to $50.
  • Intermediary banks may take a cut. A $10,000 payment might lose 1% to 2% in fees and exchange rate markups.
  • The seller's bank may hold the funds for review, especially for international payments. This can add days or weeks to the settlement time.
  • For larger orders, the buyer may need a letter of credit, which costs hundreds of dollars and requires significant paperwork.

For small businesses like Linh's, these costs and delays are a significant burden. A 2% loss on a $10,000 order is $200 — money that could go to farmers or back into the business.

The BCH way

Linh sends her customer a Bitcoin Cash address and the amount in BCH (calculated from the current exchange rate). The customer opens their wallet, scans a QR code, and sends the payment. The transaction broadcasts to the network and confirms within minutes.

The fee is a fraction of a cent. There's no intermediary, no wire transfer fee, no exchange rate markup (beyond the market rate), and no waiting. The money is in Linh's wallet almost immediately.

Linh can hold the BCH, convert it to Vietnamese dong through a local exchange, or use it to pay her farmers and suppliers who accept BCH. She has options, not obligations.

Why this matters for small businesses

For large corporations with dedicated treasury departments, the cost of bank wires is a rounding error. For small businesses operating on thin margins, it's a significant expense.

BCH offers a way to settle international payments without the banking system's friction. A small business in Vietnam can sell to a customer in the US, and the payment can settle in minutes with minimal fees. This levels the playing field for small businesses that can't afford the overhead of traditional trade finance.

The payment flow

Here's how Linh structures her invoices:

  • Quote: Linh quotes $10,000 USD for a shipment of coffee, with a note that payment is accepted in BCH at the exchange rate on the day of payment.
  • Invoice: When the customer is ready to pay, Linh sends a Bitcoin Cash address and the exact BCH amount (calculated from a public price API). She includes a 15-minute window for the rate to stay valid.
  • Payment: The customer scans a QR code or copies the address, sends the BCH, and emails a screenshot or transaction ID.
  • Confirmation: Linh checks her wallet, sees the payment, and ships the goods. The job is done.

There's no payment processor, no merchant account, no chargeback risk. The payment is final once confirmed. If Linh needs to issue a refund, she sends a new payment — simple, transparent, and under her control.

Accounting and compliance

Linh keeps records in both USD and BCH. She logs the invoice amount in USD, the BCH amount received, the exchange rate used, and the date. For tax purposes, she reports the USD value of the income on the day she received it.

She converts some BCH to Vietnamese dong immediately to cover local expenses. She holds some as a hedge against inflation and currency devaluation. Both are deliberate choices, and both work. The important thing is that Linh decides, not a bank or a payment processor.

The business owner's question

"Is this legal?" Linh checked with her accountant and lawyer. In Vietnam, as in most countries, businesses can accept cryptocurrency as payment. The key is to report the income correctly and comply with tax laws. BCH settles directly and cheaply, and the transaction is recorded on a public chain. That's a strong answer for a small business.

Privacy and security

Linh uses a new Bitcoin Cash address for each invoice. This makes it harder for anyone to link her payments together. Her wallet generates fresh addresses automatically, so she doesn't have to manage them manually.

She keeps her private keys secure, backed up on paper, and stored offline. Her wallet is watch-only on her phone — she can see incoming payments but can't spend from it. The spending keys are on a hardware wallet she only connects when she needs to move funds.

Building the flow

Linh doesn't need a complex system. She uses a wallet app on her phone, a simple invoice template (she made it in Google Docs), and a public BCH/USD price API. When she's ready to invoice, she checks the rate, calculates the BCH amount, and sends the details.

For higher volume, she could set up a small website with an invoicing tool that auto-generates addresses and locks in rates. The stickers.cash site is a working example of this kind of setup. The Cloudflare Worker that powers it is open source and can be adapted for invoicing or B2B payments.

The point is that the infrastructure is simple, cheap, and under Linh's control. She doesn't need permission to trade, and she doesn't need to pay a middleman to get paid.

Scaling the model

Linh is not unique. Small businesses around the world face the same friction: high fees, slow settlement, and limited access to banking services. Bitcoin Cash offers a way to settle international payments without those costs.

For small businesses, BCH is not just a payment method — it's a way to participate in global trade without asking permission. The contract is between the buyer and the seller, not between them and a bank.
