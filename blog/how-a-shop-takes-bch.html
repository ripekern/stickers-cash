How a shop takes BCH

Accepting BCH gives a shop a direct, final settlement path with low fees. Here is how the flow works — and why it is good for small tickets.

Post 18 of 30 · 10 min read · Oct 22, 2026

For a merchant, accepting BCH is a direct settlement path. The shop gets paid peer-to-peer, with no card network in the middle. That changes the economics of small sales, and it gives the shop a payment rail that is fast, cheap, and final.

The payment flow

A customer chooses BCH at checkout. The shop's wallet or point-of-sale app creates an invoice with an address and an amount. The customer scans a QR code, reviews the amount, and sends the payment. The shop sees the transaction broadcast, and then sees it confirmed in a block.

A good point-of-sale tool generates a fresh address for each order, watches the mempool, and marks the invoice paid when the expected amount arrives. That is a clean, auditable flow. The shop knows which order was paid, and the customer gets a clear confirmation.

Instant for small sales, confirmed for large

For small in-person purchases, the shop can hand over the goods as soon as the payment appears. For online orders or larger amounts, it can wait for one or more confirmations. The policy matches the value of the sale, and the shop stays in control of the experience.

BCH gives the merchant both options: the speed of a broadcast payment for a sticker, and the finality of a confirmed transaction for a bigger order. That flexibility is a practical advantage.

Accounting in two units

Most merchants think in local currency. The shop may price a sticker at four dollars, then convert the BCH amount at the moment of sale. The accounting record should capture both: the local-currency price, the BCH amount received, and the exchange rate used. That makes the books readable and tax reporting straightforward.

If the shop keeps the BCH, it holds a scarce asset. If it converts immediately, it uses BCH as a settlement rail and keeps its accounting in local currency. Both are deliberate choices, and both work. The important thing is that the shop decides before the first order, not after.

The merchant's question

It is not “do I believe in BCH?” It is “does this payment settle, and can I account for it?” BCH settles directly and cheaply, and the transaction is recorded on a public chain. That is a strong answer for a small shop.

Finality is an advantage

BCH payments are final once confirmed. That removes chargeback fraud and the fees that support the dispute system. A refund is a new payment, which is simple to send and easy to record. The shop publishes a clear refund policy, and the customer knows the terms up front.

Finality is good for a merchant who wants certainty. It also makes the checkout relationship transparent: the customer pays, the shop delivers, and both sides know where they stand.

Fees and small tickets

Card fees are painful on small sales. A thirty-cent fixed fee plus a percentage can eat a large share of a four-dollar sticker. A BCH transaction usually costs a fraction of a cent, so the economics of small digital sales look different. That is one reason stickers.cash can sell low-value items and ship them worldwide without building the fee into every order.

The network fee is not the only cost. The merchant still pays for the website, shipping, packaging, and the time to reconcile orders. The point is that the payment rail stops being the bottleneck for small transactions.

What the customer needs

The customer needs a wallet, a way to scan a QR code, and enough BCH to pay. Good checkout design hides the address format, shows a clear amount, and tells the customer what to do next. That is a small amount of friction for a payment rail that costs a fraction of a cent.

Accepting BCH is a statement that the shop is ready for internet cash. It gives customers a cheaper way to pay and gives the merchant final settlement. That is a good combination for a sticker shop — or any shop.

Building a BCH-only shop

Stickers.cash itself is a working example. The site runs on static HTML, CSS, and JavaScript hosted on GitHub Pages, with a small Cloudflare Worker that derives a fresh BCH address for each order. There is no database, no payment processor, and no monthly subscription to an e-commerce platform. The total infrastructure cost is a domain name and a few dollars a month.
