How a shop takes BCH

Accepting BCH is not a checkout plugin bolted onto a bank account. It is a different settlement path, with different risks and some real advantages.

Post 18 of 20 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

For a merchant, accepting BCH is not just adding another button to checkout. It changes who settles the payment, when the money is final, and what happens when something goes wrong. That can be an advantage or a headache, depending on how the shop is run.

The payment flow

A customer chooses BCH at checkout. The shop's wallet or point-of-sale app creates an invoice with an address and an amount. The customer scans a QR code, reviews the amount, and sends the payment. The shop sees the transaction broadcast, and then sees it confirmed in a block.

The address should be unique per order. A single static address makes reconciliation harder and leaks the shop's transaction history. A good point-of-sale tool generates a fresh address, watches the mempool, and marks the invoice paid when the expected amount arrives.

For small in-person purchases, the shop may accept 0-conf and hand over the goods immediately. For online orders or larger amounts, it can wait for one or more confirmations. The right policy depends on the value and the risk, not on a slogan.

Accounting in two units

Most merchants think in local currency. The shop may price a sticker at four dollars, then convert the BCH amount at the moment of sale. The accounting record should capture both: the local-currency price and the BCH amount received, plus the exchange rate used. That makes the books readable and the tax reporting possible.

If the shop keeps the BCH, it is now holding a volatile asset. If it converts immediately, it is using BCH as a settlement rail and keeping its accounting in local currency. Both are legitimate. The important thing is to choose deliberately instead of discovering the exposure later.

The merchant's real question

It is not “do I believe in BCH?” It is “do I believe this payment will settle, and can I account for it?” A merchant can accept BCH without making a price bet by converting at the time of sale.

No chargebacks, but also no undo

BCH payments are final once confirmed. There is no card network to reverse a transaction because the customer changed their mind or disputed the charge. For a merchant, that removes chargeback fraud and the fees that support the dispute system. It also means a refund is a new payment, not a reversal. The shop needs a refund policy and a way to send BCH back.

Customers lose the card-network safety net. That is a real trade-off. A merchant who wants trust may need clearer terms, better communication, and a reputation that substitutes for the chargeback system. Finality is not automatically good for every transaction. It is a different allocation of risk.

Fees and small tickets

Card fees are painful on small sales. A thirty-cent fixed fee plus a percentage can eat a large share of a four-dollar sticker. A BCH transaction usually costs a fraction of a cent, so the economics of small digital sales look different. That is one reason stickers.cash can sell low-value items and ship them worldwide without building the fee into every order.

The network fee is not the only cost. The merchant still pays for the website, shipping, packaging, and the time to reconcile orders. The point is that the payment rail stops being the bottleneck for small transactions.

What the customer needs

The customer needs a wallet, a way to scan a QR code, and enough BCH to pay. That is a higher bar than tapping a card. Good checkout design hides the address format, shows a clear amount, and tells the customer what to do next. Bad design shows a long string and hopes for the best.

Accepting BCH is a statement that the shop is willing to meet customers where they are. It is not a promise that every customer will use it. The merchant's job is to make the path clear enough that the people who do use it are not punished for choosing a cheaper rail.
