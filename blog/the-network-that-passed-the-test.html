The network that passed the test

Bitcoin Cash has been deliberately flooded. The result was not a fee spike or a frozen mempool. It was a demonstration of what on-chain capacity is for.

Post 20 of 30 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Scaling claims are easy to make. Anyone can say their network will handle the world's payments. A stress test is harder to fake: you flood the network, watch the blocks, watch the fees, and see whether the thing actually clears.

September 2018: 2.1 million transactions

In September 2018, the BCH community ran a coordinated stress test on the mainnet. The goal was to push millions of minimum-fee transactions through the network in 24 hours and see what broke. More than 2.1 million transactions were processed.

The result was not a fee spike. Reports at the time showed average fees staying around a fraction of a cent, actually dipping from roughly $0.002 to $0.0017. Blocks kept being mined. The chain kept moving. The queue did not turn into a permanent auction.

That test mattered because it was not a simulation of a happy path. It was a deliberate attempt to fill the network with low-value traffic. The network was asked to do the thing big blocks are supposed to do: carry volume without making every payment expensive.

2021: the load was not synthetic

Stress tests are controlled. Real usage is not. In early 2021, BCH processed more than 300,000 transactions in a day, and miners produced multi-megabyte blocks, including a 4.7 MB block with nearly 15,000 transactions. Fees stayed around a fraction of a cent.

That is not a claim that BCH was overloaded. It is evidence that larger blocks were not theoretical. Miners included them. Nodes validated them. The network settled them. The capacity was used in the wild, not only in a lab.

2024: testing the limit itself

The Adaptive Block Limit Algorithm added a new question: what happens when demand keeps rising for years? The answer was modeled before it was needed. ABLA stress-test simulations run sustained demand through the algorithm and watch whether the limit rises fast enough to keep blocks from staying full.

The goal of the simulation is specific: prevent sustained congestion, prevent fee spikes, and keep the mempool clear as usage grows. The limit moves gradually, so the network is not asked to absorb an instant jump. The 32 MB floor remains underneath.

A simulation is not a guarantee. It is a test of the mechanism. The mechanism exists so that capacity can respond to demand without a recurring political fight over a fixed number.

What the tests actually prove

They prove that BCH can process large blocks, that miners will mine them, that nodes will validate them, and that fees do not automatically explode when the network is busy. They prove that a payment network can be designed to clear a queue rather than ration it.

They do not prove that everyone will show up. They do not prove that adoption will be fast, that wallets will be easy, or that the price will be stable. Those are different problems. The engineering question — can the rails carry volume without collapsing into a fee market? — has been answered in the affirmative.

The point of the test

A payment network is not proven by a white paper. It is proven by what happens when traffic arrives. BCH has been flooded on purpose, has carried real bursts of usage, and has modeled sustained demand through its adaptive limit. In each case, the design did what it was supposed to do: keep blocks moving and keep fees low.

That does not mean the work is finished. It means the capacity argument is no longer a hypothesis. The network can clear the queue. The remaining challenge is giving the world a reason to fill it.
