Cross-border remittances with BCH

A worker in the US sends money home to family in the Philippines. The payment settles in minutes, costs pennies, and no one needs a bank account or approval.

Post 33 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Juan works construction in California. His family lives in a small town in the Philippines. Every month, he sends money home to help with rent, food, and his younger sister's school fees. He's been doing this for five years.

For the first three years, he used Western Union. He'd pay $200 and his family would receive about $180, after fees and exchange rate markups. The money would take two to three days to arrive, and his mother would have to travel to the nearest town to pick it up.

Then Juan learned about Bitcoin Cash. Now he sends $200 in BCH, and his family receives $199.50 or more. The payment settles in minutes, and his mother can cash out at a local exchange or use a BCH-friendly merchant in town.

The old way

Traditional remittance services charge high fees and offer poor exchange rates. For a $200 transfer from the US to the Philippines, the total cost can be 5% to 10% when you add up the transfer fee, the exchange rate markup, and any intermediary bank fees.

The transfer takes days, not minutes. The recipient often has to travel to a pickup location, present ID, and wait in line. For families in rural areas, this is a significant burden.

Worse, the sender has no visibility into the process. Once the money is handed to Western Union or MoneyGram, it's out of their hands. They can track the transfer with a reference number, but they can't verify the exchange rate or see where the money is in the pipeline.

The BCH way

Juan opens his BCH wallet and enters his mother's address. He checks the current exchange rate, calculates the BCH amount, and sends the payment. The transaction broadcasts to the network and confirms within minutes.

The fee is a fraction of a cent. There's no intermediary, no exchange rate markup (beyond the market rate Juan sees), and no waiting. The money is in his mother's wallet almost immediately.

His mother can hold the BCH, convert it to pesos at a local exchange, or use it directly to buy goods from a merchant who accepts BCH. She has options, not obligations.

Why this matters

Global remittances are a $700 billion+ industry. For many developing countries, remittances are a significant portion of GDP. The Philippines receives over $30 billion per year in remittances — about 8% of its GDP.

Traditional remittance services extract billions in fees. Bitcoin Cash offers a way to send money across borders without those costs. For a family depending on that money, the difference between receiving $180 and $200 is not abstract — it's food, medicine, and school fees.

The remittance flow

Here's how Juan sends money home:

  • Step 1: Juan checks the BCH/USD exchange rate. He decides to send $200 worth of BCH.
  • Step 2: He opens his wallet, enters his mother's BCH address, and calculates the amount. His wallet shows the current rate and the exact BCH amount.
  • Step 3: He sends the payment. The transaction broadcasts and confirms within minutes.
  • Step 4: His mother receives a notification on her phone. She can check her wallet to see the balance, or use a block explorer to verify the transaction.
  • Step 5: She decides what to do with the BCH. She can hold it, convert it to pesos, or spend it directly.

The whole process takes less than 10 minutes. The fee is a fraction of a cent. There's no paperwork, no ID required, no travel to a pickup location.

Privacy and control

Juan and his mother both use wallets they control. They hold their own keys. No company can freeze their funds, block their transactions, or demand identification.

The transactions are public on the blockchain, but the addresses are pseudonymous. Unless Juan's mother's identity is linked to her address, no one knows who she is or where she lives. She uses a fresh address for each payment, so her transaction history is not easily linkable.

Converting to local currency

Juan's mother needs pesos to pay for everyday expenses. She has a few options:

  • Local exchange: She can use a BCH-friendly exchange that operates in the Philippines. Some exchanges allow cash withdrawals or bank transfers.
  • Peer-to-peer: She can find someone in her town who wants to buy BCH for pesos. This is direct, peer-to-peer, and often offers better rates than an exchange.
  • Direct spending: If there are merchants in her town who accept BCH, she can spend it directly. This is less common but growing.

The key point is that she has choices. She's not locked into a single service with high fees and limited access.

The family's question

"Is this safe?" Juan's mother was skeptical at first. But she can see the transaction on the blockchain, she controls her wallet, and the money arrives in minutes. She doesn't need to trust a company — she just needs to trust the network and her own keys.

Scaling the model

Juan is not unique. Millions of workers send money home every month. If even a small fraction of those remittances moved through BCH, the savings would be enormous — not just for the families, but for the economies of the receiving countries.

The infrastructure is already there. Wallets, exchanges, and merchants exist in many countries. The challenge is adoption and education, not technology.

For workers like Juan, BCH is not just a payment method — it's a way to support their families without the friction and cost of traditional remittance services. It's internet cash, doing what cash should do: move freely, cheaply, and directly from person to person.
