Cross-border remittances with BCH

A worker in the US sends money home to family in the Philippines. The payment settles in minutes, costs almost nothing, and no one needs a traditional bank account or approval.

Post 32 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Juan didn't set out to become part of a financial revolution. He was just trying to send money home to his mother in the Philippines — and getting hit with fees that made no sense.

Three years ago, he was paying Western Union $200 so his family could receive $180. That's 10% gone before the money even left the counter. He'd stand in line, fill out forms, and wait two to three days for his mother to travel to town and pick up the cash.

Then he learned about Bitcoin Cash.

The first transfer

Juan downloaded a wallet on his phone and walked into a local Bitcoin Cash exchange. He bought 1,000,000 satoshis — about $200 — and sent them to his mother's wallet. The transaction confirmed in minutes. His mother received 999,000 sats. The network fee was less than a penny.

He stared at his phone, waiting for something to go wrong. Nothing did.

"I thought I was doing something illegal," he says. "But I was just sending money. Faster, cheaper, and with nobody taking a cut."

Building the habit

The first few months, Juan was cautious. He'd send small amounts, check the blockchain to make sure the transaction went through, then have his mother confirm she received it. She'd cash out at a local exchange or spend directly with merchants who accepted BCH.

By month six, Juan had a routine. Every payday, he'd convert $200 to BCH and send it home. The fees were still less than a penny. His mother still received 99.9% of what he sent. She never had to leave town.

By year one, Juan had sent over $2,400 home. He'd saved $240 in fees — money that went toward his mother's medical bills, his sister's school fees, and groceries.

The network effect

Juan's coworkers noticed. They'd been using Western Union and MoneyGram for years, paying the same 5-10% fees. He showed them how BCH worked. They downloaded wallets, learned to convert, and started sending money home themselves.

Within a year, Juan's construction crew had sent over $50,000 in BCH. They'd saved more than $5,000 in fees. Some of them had started holding a little BCH as savings, not just for sending home.

"It's like we built our own bank," Juan says. "But it's not a bank. It's just us, sending money to the people we love."

The bigger picture

Juan's story is not unique. It's happening everywhere. Workers in the US sending money to Mexico. Teachers in London sending money to Nigeria. Engineers in Dubai sending money to India. The remittance market is $700 billion per year, and it's built on slow, expensive, opaque systems.

BCH doesn't just save money. It saves time. It gives recipients control. It's transparent, fast, and works even when banks don't. For families who depend on remittances, it's not a nice-to-have. It's a lifeline.

Juan knows this better than anyone. His mother's medical bills are paid. His sister is in school. And every month, when Juan sends money home, he's not just sending cash. He's sending hope.

"I used to think money was just money," he says. "Now I know it's a tool. And BCH is the best tool I've ever used."

And individuals aren't the only ones crossing borders. A small business using BCH for international trade shows how a Vietnamese coffee exporter sells to a US roaster — settling in minutes, for pennies, with no bank in the middle.
