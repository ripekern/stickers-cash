A local community building BCH commerce

A neighborhood in El Salvador builds a local economy around BCH. Coffee shops, mechanics, and market vendors accept it. The community owns the payment rail.

Post 34 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Walk down the main street of El Carmen, a small town outside Santa Tecla, El Salvador, and you'll see the stickers. In the window of the coffee shop. On the counter at the mechanic. Taped to the register at the market. "Bitcoin Cash accepted here."

Three years ago, there were zero. Today, there are 23 merchants accepting BCH in a town of 8,000 people. The coffee shop has processed over 2,000 BCH transactions. The mechanic accepts it for oil changes and brake jobs. The market vendors take it for produce. The hair salon takes it for cuts. A customer can walk down the street and pay for everything — breakfast, a haircut, groceries, car repair — without touching a bank account.

This didn't happen by accident. It happened because two friends decided to try something.

The spark

In 2023, Carlos owned a coffee shop and Miguel ran a garage next door. They'd been friends for 20 years. They'd also both been burned by banks — Carlos lost a month's revenue when his account was frozen for "suspicious activity" (he'd deposited cash from a weekend festival). Miguel paid $400 in wire transfer fees to pay a supplier in Guatemala.

They heard about Bitcoin Cash at a meetup in San Salvador. A guy from the capital showed them how it worked. "You send it like a text message," he said. "The fee is less than a penny. No bank, no processor, no middleman."

Carlos and Miguel looked at each other. "Let's try it," Carlos said.

They downloaded wallets, printed QR codes, and taped them to their counters. They told their regulars. "If you have BCH, you can pay with it. No fees, no bullshit."

First blood

The first transaction came three weeks later. A guy from the capital stopped in for coffee, saw the QR code, and asked if it was real. Carlos said yes. The guy scanned it with his phone, sent 50,000 satoshis (about $3), and Carlos's phone buzzed. Payment received.

"I just stood there looking at my phone," Carlos says. "Three dollars. I kept three dollars. Not $2.85 after fees. Three dollars. And it settled in two seconds."

The next day, a customer paid Miguel for an oil change in BCH. 500,000 satoshis, about $30. Miguel's phone buzzed. Payment received. No invoice, no receipt, no waiting for the bank to clear it.

"It felt like magic," Miguel says. "But it wasn't magic. It was just cash. Digital cash."

Word spreads

Carlos told the market vendors. "You pay 3% to the card company? I pay zero." Miguel told the hair salon. "You wait three days for the bank to settle? I get it instantly."

Within two months, five more merchants had BCH on their counters. They printed stickers, shared wallet addresses, and helped each other set up. They didn't need a payment processor. They didn't need a merchant account. They didn't need permission.

They created a WhatsApp group to share tips. "How do you convert to colones?" "What's the best wallet?" "How do you handle refunds?" They learned together. When one merchant had a problem, the others helped solve it.

Building the infrastructure

By month six, they had 12 merchants. A customer could buy coffee, get a haircut, pick up groceries, and pay for car repairs — all in BCH. But there was a problem: most customers didn't have BCH.

So they started educating. Carlos put a sign in his window: "Bitcoin Cash workshops every Saturday. Learn how to set up a wallet in 10 minutes." Miguel hosted a session at his garage. "Bring your phone, leave with a wallet."

They created a simple directory — a printed map of BCH-accepting merchants. They handed it out at the market. "You can pay for everything on this street with BCH."

They also started a local exchange. A guy from the capital came to town once a week with cash. Merchants could sell him BCH for colones. He'd wire the colones to their bank accounts. It wasn't perfect, but it worked.

The challenges

It hasn't been easy. In 2024, BCH dropped 30% in a month. Merchants who were holding it lost money. They learned to convert quickly or only hold what they needed for expenses.

Customer adoption is still slow. Most people in El Carmen still pay in cash or card. The merchants accept BCH, but it's only 15% of their revenue. They're working on it — more education, more outreach, more workshops.

Regulation is unclear. El Salvador has embraced Bitcoin broadly, but the rules around BCH are still evolving. The merchants stay informed, work with local authorities, and operate transparently. They're not trying to break the law. They're just trying to own their payment rail.

What it looks like now

Today, El Carmen has 23 BCH-accepting merchants. The coffee shop processes 200+ transactions per month. The mechanic accepts BCH for everything from oil changes to engine rebuilds. The market vendors take it for produce. The hair salon takes it for cuts.

The community has built something together. They didn't wait for a government or a corporation to give them a payment system. They built it themselves, peer to peer, using a protocol that's open to anyone.

"We own our payment rail," Carlos says. "No one can freeze it. No one can charge us fees. No one can shut it down. It's ours."

The merchant's question

"Do I need to understand the technology?" No. You need a wallet, a QR code, and a way to check your balance. The rest is just like cash — you receive it, you hold it, you spend it, or you convert it. The protocol handles the rest.

The lesson

The model is simple: start small, prove it works, grow the network. The technology is already there. The challenge is coordination — getting merchants to adopt, educating customers, and building the infrastructure.

El Carmen didn't try to build a full economy overnight. They started with two merchants. They proved it worked. Then they grew. They didn't need a payment processor or a bank. They needed trust, education, and a shared commitment to owning their payment rail.

This is not unique to El Salvador. Communities around the world are building local BCH economies. In Venezuela, merchants use BCH to bypass inflation. In the Philippines, workers use it to receive remittances. In Nigeria, traders use it to settle cross-border payments.

The model is the same: start small, prove it works, grow the network. The technology is already there. The challenge is adoption and education.

For communities that want to own their payment rail, BCH offers a way. It's about ownership, sovereignty, and the ability to transact without asking permission.

Freelancers, nonprofits, remittances, businesses, creators, and communities — all on board. Bitcoin Cash is everywhere brings it all together: the summary, the use cases, and the next step.
