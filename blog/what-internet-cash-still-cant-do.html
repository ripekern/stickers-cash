What internet cash still can't do

BCH fixes the middleman problem. It does not fix every money problem. An honest map of what is still missing — and what has to be built next.

Post 20 of 20 · 4 min read · Oct 22, 2026

The case for internet cash is strong, but it is not a claim that BCH solves every problem. A useful guide has to include the limits. Here is what Bitcoin Cash still cannot do well, and what has to be built next.

It is not yet normal money

Most people do not have a BCH wallet. Most merchants do not accept it. Most employers do not pay in it. A currency becomes useful when it is widely accepted, and BCH is still building that network. A sticker shop can accept it. A local cafe might. A landlord probably will not.

This is the classic chicken-and-egg problem. Merchants want customers. Customers want places to spend. Communities can bootstrap both, but bootstrapping is slower than a protocol upgrade. No amount of technical elegance replaces the boring work of adoption.

It is not a stable unit of account

Prices are still set in local currency. BCH swings against those currencies, so a wallet balance can change in purchasing-power terms while the number of coins stays fixed. That makes BCH hard to use for long-term budgeting, payroll, or any contract that needs a stable unit.

Stablecoins and layered systems try to solve this, but they introduce their own trust assumptions. BCH itself does not promise a stable price. It promises a fixed supply and a payment rail. Those are different things.

The user experience is still too sharp

Seed phrases, address formats, confirmations, backups, and wallet recovery are not normal consumer experiences. A person who has never used a bank app can learn them, but most people should not have to. A good wallet should make the safe path the easy path.

That means better recovery, clearer warnings, safer address handling, and fewer places where a small mistake loses money forever. It also means better support. “Read the documentation” is not a support strategy for a payment network that wants ordinary users.

No safety net by default

There is no FDIC insurance, no chargeback department, and no password reset. That is a feature for censorship resistance and a bug for consumer protection. The ecosystem has to build trustworthy services and clear expectations without recreating the middlemen it replaced.

Regulation and privacy live at the edges

BCH itself is a protocol, but the places where people buy, sell, and spend it are regulated businesses. Identity checks, reporting requirements, and tax rules vary by country. A self-custodial wallet can avoid a bank, but it cannot avoid the law or the records created by an exchange.

The public ledger is also not private by default. Address hygiene helps, but chain analysis, network surveillance, and identity-linked on-ramps can still connect transactions to people. Privacy is a practice and an ongoing area of development, not a solved feature.

Security has to be paid for

Proof-of-work security depends on miner revenue. The block subsidy shrinks over time. If fees stay tiny forever, the long-term security budget becomes a serious question. Low fees are good for payments, but they are not automatically good for security. The network needs enough usage and value to keep miners honest.

This is not a BCH-only problem. Every capped-supply proof-of-work chain faces a version of it. The answer will be some mix of usage, fee revenue, and whatever the market decides the chain is worth securing.

What is left to build

The list is long, and none of it is glamorous: wallets that ordinary people can recover, merchant tools that reconcile automatically, better privacy, clearer tax guidance, and more places to spend. The protocol is the easy part compared with changing habits.

That is not a reason to wait. It is a reason to be precise about what you are using. BCH is already useful for people who hold their own keys, accept the volatility, and have somewhere to spend it. It is not yet useful for everyone, and pretending otherwise makes the guide less trustworthy.

The first chapter explained why the ice is thin. This chapter explained how the new ground works. The rest is about making it normal — one wallet, one merchant, and one payment at a time.
