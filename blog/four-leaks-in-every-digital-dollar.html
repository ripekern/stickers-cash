Four leaks in every digital dollar

Watched, freezable, fee-taxed, and melting. The deeper leak is the one most people never see: banks create the money they lend, and the central bank creates the reserves underneath it.

Post 2 of 30 · 9 min read · Oct 22, 2026

That means today, every digital dollar you have is:

👁️ Watched

Bought, searched, and sold as data, leaked in hacks you can't prevent.

🔒 Permissioned

Freezable, blockable, and reversible. A payment flagged as fraud, an account closed by an algorithm, a business de-banked overnight — anyone outside the approved pattern learns this first.

💸 Taxed by fees

$0.30 + 3% makes a $1 digital sale impossible, and cross-border $5 transfers cost $2 and take days.

🧊 Melting

Inflation quietly steals purchasing power while your bank pays you 0.01%.

Slowly at first, then all at once, the ice cracks. An account locked the day rent is due. A payment flagged for fraud while traveling. A business de-banked overnight. Savings that buy less every year. This is the route most people are on, and they don't see it until they're already in cold water.

Those four leaks are real. But they are symptoms. To understand why they exist, you have to understand two things: how the dollar became digital, and how banks create the money they lend.

When the dollar became digital

The dollar did not become digital on one day. It became digital one layer at a time: the Federal Reserve in 1913, the gold link broken in 1933, Bretton Woods in 1944, Nixon closing the gold window in 1971, and quantitative easing after 2008. By 2023, when the Fed launched FedNow, most money was already digital—just ledger entries in bank databases.

The transition from paper to pixels changed everything. Paper cash is final, private, and needs no permission. Digital dollars are watched, freezable, and reversible. The paper in your wallet is a small minority of the money supply. The rest is a stack of IOUs on screens. Read the deep dive on the dollar's digital transition →

Why we stay in this system

If the system has these costs, why does everyone use it? Because it is not a personal choice. It is a coordination system. Taxes, payroll, mortgages, and rent are all in dollars. Your employer, landlord, and grocery store all accept the same unit. Deposit insurance protects your balance. The system works well enough for most people most of the time.

The people it fails are the ones who need final payment, privacy, or an exit the most. But leaving means changing how you are paid, how you save, and how you pay taxes. That is a coordination problem, not a weekend project.

How banks create money

This is the part that sounds impossible, but it is true. Banks do not print paper dollars. They create deposits—the digital money in your account—when they make loans. The loan and the deposit are created together, on the bank's balance sheet. The Bank of England says it plainly: "Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit." Read the deep dive on how banks create money →

Who gets the new money first

New money does not arrive evenly. It enters through the banking system and financial markets. The banks, bond sellers, and borrowers closest to the creation point can spend it before prices fully adjust. Everyone else meets it later as higher prices. That is not a secret plot; it is what happens when new claims are injected at specific points in a network. This is called the Cantillon effect, and it turns a technical fact into a moral question.
