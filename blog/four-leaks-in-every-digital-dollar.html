Four leaks in every digital dollar

Watched, freezable, fee-taxed, and melting. The deeper leak is the one most people never see: banks create the money they lend, and the central bank creates the reserves underneath it.

Post 2 of 30 · 9 min read · Oct 22, 2026

That means today, every digital dollar you have is:

👁️ Watched

Bought, searched, and sold as data, leaked in hacks you can't prevent.

🔒 Permissioned

Freezable, blockable, and reversible. A payment flagged as fraud, an account closed by an algorithm, a business de-banked overnight — anyone outside the approved pattern learns this first.

💸 Taxed by fees

$0.30 + 3% makes a $1 digital sale impossible, and cross-border $5 transfers cost $2 and take days.

🧊 Melting

Inflation quietly steals purchasing power while your bank pays you 0.01%.

Slowly at first, then all at once, the ice cracks. An account locked the day rent is due. A payment flagged for fraud while traveling. A business de-banked overnight. Savings that buy less every year. This is the route most people are on, and they don't see it until they're already in cold water.

Those four leaks are real. But they are symptoms. To understand why they exist, you have to go back to the moment the dollar became a digital claim—and to the rule that lets banks create those claims.

When the dollar became digital

The dollar did not become digital on one day. It became digital one layer at a time, until the paper was mostly a wrapper around a ledger.

  1. 1913 — a central bank. The Federal Reserve Act created the Fed and a new national currency system. The Fed could create reserves and lend in a panic.
  2. 1933–34 — the domestic gold link breaks. In the Depression, the government stopped Americans from redeeming dollars for gold and then devalued the dollar against gold. The dollar was still linked internationally, but at home it was becoming a managed claim.
  3. 1944 — Bretton Woods. The postwar system tied other currencies to the dollar, and the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce. The dollar became the world's reserve currency.
  4. 1971 — the gold window closes. President Nixon suspended the conversion of dollars into gold. The Fed's own history says the international monetary system "turned into a fiat one." From then on, the dollar's supply was backed by the government's authority and the Fed's management, not by a fixed weight of metal.
  5. 2008 and 2020 — digital reserves at scale. During the financial crisis and the pandemic, the Fed bought trillions in bonds and paid with newly created reserves. The balance sheet went from about $0.9 trillion in 2007 to $6.76 trillion in October 2026.
  6. 2023 — instant settlement. The Fed launched FedNow, a real-time payment system. It made bank money faster. It did not make it cash.

Today the Fed itself says Americans "have long held money predominantly in digital form—for example in bank accounts, payment apps or through online transactions." Its 2022 payments paper puts it more plainly: "Commercial bank money is the digital form of money that is most commonly used by the public."

That is the system you use every day. Your paycheck is a deposit. Your Venmo balance is a deposit or a claim on one. Your debit card is an instruction to move a deposit. The paper bills in your wallet are a small minority of the money supply. The rest is a stack of IOUs on screens.

Why we stay in this system

If the system has these costs, why does everyone use it? Because it is not a personal choice. It is a coordination system, and leaving it alone is expensive.

  • It is the unit of account. Taxes, payroll, mortgages, rent, accounting, and debt are denominated in dollars. You can hold another asset, but a taxpayer still has to settle their tax bill in dollars.
  • It has network effects. Your employer, landlord, bank, doctor, and grocery store all accept the same unit. A currency is valuable because other people accept it, and they accept it because it is valuable.
  • It is insured and backstopped. Deposit insurance protects bank balances up to legal limits. The Fed can lend to banks in a panic. That makes bank money feel like cash, even though it is a promise.
  • It is convenient. Direct deposit, cards, automatic bill pay, credit, and fraud protection are real benefits. Most people never see the plumbing, so they never price it.
  • It is the path of least resistance. You were born into it. Your contracts are written in it. Switching means changing how you are paid, how you save, and how you pay taxes. That is a coordination problem, not a weekend project.

None of that makes the costs disappear. It just makes them easy to ignore. The system works well enough for most people most of the time. The people it fails are the ones who need final payment, privacy, or an exit the most.

Not a conspiracy. A design.

The Fed is not hidden. It publishes its balance sheet, minutes, and financial statements. The problem is structural: a government-backed unit of account, a central bank that can create reserves, and a banking system that can create deposits. You can see every part of it and still be subject to it.

How banks create money

This is the part that sounds impossible. It is also the part with the clearest official proof.

Banks do not print paper dollars. They create deposits—the digital money in your account—when they make loans. The loan and the deposit are created together, on the bank's balance sheet.

What happensYouThe bank
You sign a $300,000 mortgage.You owe $300,000 plus interest.It records a $300,000 loan as an asset.
The bank credits your account.You have a $300,000 deposit.It records a $300,000 deposit as a liability.

The bank did not wait for a saver to deposit $300,000. It created the deposit and the loan at the same time. That is not a metaphor. The Bank of England says it plainly:

"Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money."

— Bank of England, "Money creation in the modern economy" (2014)

So is it unlimited? Not literally. Banks are constrained by capital, liquidity, regulation, and the demand for credit. A bank cannot create loans forever without losing solvency. But there is no fixed pile of gold or paper setting the ceiling. The limit is policy, profit, and demand—not a physical quantity of money.

The central bank can create the reserves that sit underneath the system. When the Fed buys a bond, it pays with new reserves. It does not need to collect taxes or borrow from savers first. That is why the Fed's balance sheet can expand from about $0.9 trillion in 2007 to $6.76 trillion in October 2026.

This is the modern version of issuing currency. Banks issue deposits. The central bank issues reserves. The Treasury prints paper. All three are claims backed by rules and institutions rather than by a fixed weight of metal. The digital ones are ledger entries.

Who gets the new money first

New money does not arrive evenly. It enters through the banking system and financial markets. The banks, bond sellers, and borrowers closest to the creation point can spend it before prices fully adjust. Everyone else meets it later as higher prices. That is not a secret plot; it is what happens when new claims are injected at specific points in a network.

This is the part that turns a technical fact into a moral question. If money is created by banks and central banks, then the first users get an advantage that savers and wage earners do not. You do not have to be a conspiracy theorist to see it. You just have to follow the ledger.

Why it feels unbelievable

You were taught that money is earned. You work, you get paid, you save. That is how most people experience money, and it is true for your paycheck. It is not how the banking system creates money.

The bank does not produce the house. It creates the claim that buys the house, then charges interest on the claim. The borrower works to repay. The bank earns the spread. The bank is not doing nothing—it is taking credit risk, doing paperwork, and providing a service—but it is not working for the money in the way you do. It is creating the ledger entry and renting it out.

That is why the truth feels unbelievable. It conflicts with the intuition that money must be a thing you earn. In a digital system, money is a ledger entry. The rules decide who can create entries. The people who write and enforce the rules have a power that most people never think about.

The point

You are not crazy for thinking you should work for your money. The system is doing something your intuition was never taught. Once you see it, the question changes: who gets to create the ledger entries, and who pays for them?
