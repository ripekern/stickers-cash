One transaction, end to end

A payment is not a message to a bank. It is a signed instruction that spends specific coins and creates new ones. Here is the whole trip.

Post 12 of 20 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Imagine you buy a four-dollar sticker. You open your wallet, scan the shop's address, type the amount, and tap send. A second later the shop's screen says the payment arrived. What actually happened in between?

Your wallet picks the bills

BCH does not work like a bank balance that gets debited from the top. It works with unspent transaction outputs, or UTXOs. Think of them as bills and coins in a drawer. If you need to pay four dollars, the wallet may pick a ten-dollar output and create two new outputs: four dollars to the shop, and six dollars back to you as change.

That is why a transaction has inputs and outputs. The inputs are the existing outputs being spent. The outputs are the new locks being created. The difference between the total input and total output is the fee, which the miner can claim.

The wallet does this automatically. You usually see an amount and an address, not the plumbing. But the plumbing explains several things: why a transaction can have multiple inputs, why “change” is a real output, and why the fee depends on the size of the transaction rather than the dollar amount.

The signature is the authorization

Each input contains a lock that only the owner of a particular key can satisfy. Your wallet builds the transaction, signs it with your private key, and broadcasts it to the network. Nodes check the signature and the rules. If the signature is wrong, the transaction is rejected. If it is right, the nodes pass it along.

No bank approves it. No card network authorizes it. The signature is the authorization. That is the whole point of peer-to-peer cash: the person receiving the money can verify the payment without asking a middleman for permission.

The mempool and the block

A broadcast transaction lands in the mempool, the holding area where nodes keep valid transactions that have not yet been mined. Miners choose transactions from the mempool, usually prioritizing by fee per byte, and assemble them into a block.

When a miner finds a valid block, the transaction becomes part of the chain. That is one confirmation. Each later block adds another. The shop can see the payment before it is confirmed, which is why small purchases often feel instant. For larger amounts, waiting for at least one confirmation is the normal safety step.

What the public sees

Anyone can look up the transaction on a block explorer. They can see the inputs, the outputs, the amount, the fee, and the block it landed in. They do not automatically see your name. The ledger is public and pseudonymous, not a public directory of identities.

That transparency is useful. It lets the receiver verify that the payment exists and that the rules were followed. It also means privacy depends on how you use addresses, which is a topic of its own.

The short version

A BCH payment is a signed transfer of control over specific coins. The wallet selects inputs, creates outputs, signs, and broadcasts. Nodes verify. Miners include. The receiver can verify the result without a bank.

The first time you send BCH, send a small amount to your own second wallet. Watch it move. Look at the transaction before and after confirmation. The abstraction becomes much less mysterious once you have seen one payment make the whole trip.
