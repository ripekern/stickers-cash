One transaction, end to end

A payment is not a message to a bank. It is a signed instruction that spends specific coins and creates new ones. Here is the whole trip.

Post 11 of 37 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Imagine you buy a four-dollar sticker. You open your wallet, scan the shop's address, type the amount, and tap send. A second later the shop's screen says the payment arrived. What actually happened in between?

Your wallet picks the bills

BCH does not work like a bank balance that gets debited from the top. It works more like a drawer of bills and coins. If you need to pay four dollars, the wallet may pick a ten-dollar bill and create two new pieces: four dollars to the shop, and six dollars back to you as change.

That is why a transaction has inputs (what you are spending) and outputs (what you are creating). The difference between the total input and total output is the fee, which goes to the miner who processes the transaction.

The wallet does this automatically. You usually see an amount and an address, not the plumbing. But the plumbing explains several things: why a transaction can have multiple inputs, why "change" is real, and why the fee depends on the size of the transaction rather than the dollar amount.

The signature is the approval

Each input has a lock that only your key can open. Your wallet builds the transaction, signs it with your private key, and sends it out to the network. Computers across the network check the signature and the rules. If the signature is wrong, the transaction is rejected. If it is right, it gets passed along.

No bank approves it. No card network authorizes it. The signature is the approval. That is the whole point of peer-to-peer cash: the person receiving the money can verify the payment without asking a middleman for permission.

After the signature is verified, the transaction enters the waiting area and then gets added to a block — the final step that makes it permanent.

What the public sees

Anyone can look up the transaction on a block explorer. They can see the inputs, the outputs, the amount, the fee, and the block it landed in. They do not automatically see your name. The ledger is public, but it shows addresses rather than identities.

That transparency is useful. It lets the receiver verify that the payment exists and that the rules were followed. It also means privacy depends on how you use addresses, which is a topic of its own.

The short version

A BCH payment is a signed transfer of control over specific coins. The wallet picks which coins to spend, creates new ones for the receiver and for change, signs, and sends. Network computers verify. Miners add to the chain. The receiver can verify the payment and spend it right away — no bank, no waiting.

The first time you send BCH, send a small amount to your own second wallet. Watch it move. Look at the transaction before and after confirmation. The abstraction becomes much less mysterious once you have seen one payment make the whole trip. This is how BCH proves itself: not through arguments or whitepapers, but through the experience of actually using it. One transaction is all it takes to see that this works. The technical details — how coins are tracked, how transactions wait for confirmation, and how blocks are built — are all part of the model.

And when that transaction reaches the receiver? It's already there — and settling in a block is not a delay, it's certainty. Fast payments, final settlement explains why speed and finality are not the same thing.
