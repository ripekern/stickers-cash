Online creators monetizing with BCH

A musician in Brazil sells directly to fans worldwide. The payments settle in minutes, cost almost nothing, and no platform takes a cut.

Post 33 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Rafael didn't start making music to get rich. He started making music because he had something to say, and São Paulo gave him a lot to say about.

By 2024, he had three albums on Bandcamp, a growing email list, and fans in 40 countries. He was also broke. After Bandcamp's 15% cut, payment processing fees, and currency conversion markups, he was making less than $3 per album sold. His fans in Japan and Germany were paying $10, but by the time the money reached his bank account in Brazil, it was $6.50.

"I'm not complaining," he says. "I love making music. But I was working 40 hours a week at a call center just to afford studio time. The music was free, but the rent wasn't."

The breaking point

In March 2025, Rafael's hard drive crashed. He lost two years of unreleased work. When he finally got it recovered, the bill was $400 — more than he'd made from music sales in the previous quarter.

That night, he sat in his studio and looked at his Bandcamp dashboard. He'd sold 127 albums that month. After fees, he'd made $380. The hard drive repair cost more than a month of music sales.

"I thought, there has to be a better way. I'm not asking for much. I just want to keep the money my fans are sending me."

Finding BCH

A friend told him about Bitcoin Cash. "You can sell directly," she said. "No platform, no fees. The money goes straight to your wallet."

Rafael was skeptical. "I'm a musician, not a tech guy. I don't want to deal with crypto."

But his friend showed him how simple it was. She'd set up a small website that generated BCH addresses for each sale. When a fan bought an album, they'd get a QR code, scan it with their wallet, and the payment would settle in minutes. The network fee was less than a penny. Rafael kept 100% of the sale price.

"I thought, how hard can it be? I can build a website. I can learn this."

The first sale

Rafael spent a weekend building a simple site. He uploaded his albums, wrote descriptions, and set prices. He added a note: "Now accepting Bitcoin Cash. 100% of the sale goes directly to the artist."

He sent an email to his list. "I'm trying something new. If you want to support me directly, you can buy my music with BCH. No platform fees, no middlemen. Just you and me."

The first sale came three hours later. A fan in Berlin bought the latest album for $10. Rafael watched the payment settle in his wallet. The network fee was $0.0003. He kept $9.9997.

"I stared at the screen for a long time," he says. "Ten dollars. I kept ten dollars. Not $8.50, not $6.50. Ten dollars."

Building momentum

Over the next month, Rafael made 43 sales through his site. He kept $430. On Bandcamp, those same sales would have netted him $300. The difference was $130 — enough to cover a week of studio time.

But the real difference was the relationship. When fans bought directly, they gave him their email. They sent him messages. They told him what they thought of the music. They asked when the next album was coming.

"On Bandcamp, I had no idea who was buying my music," he says. "Now I have a list of people who care enough to support me directly. These are my real fans. And I can talk to them."

The technical setup

Rafael's site is simple. It's a static HTML site hosted on GitHub Pages. When a fan clicks "Buy with BCH," a Cloudflare Worker generates a fresh address and displays a QR code. After payment, the fan gets a download link.

He uses an xpub to derive addresses, so he never exposes his private keys. He logs each sale for tax purposes. He keeps some BCH, converts some to reais for rent. He's learning as he goes.

"I'm not a programmer," he says. "But I'm a musician. I can learn new things. This is just another instrument."

What changed

Six months later, Rafael is making $800 per month from direct sales. That's not enough to quit his day job, but it's enough to cover studio time, equipment, and living expenses. He's released two new albums. He's built a community of 200 fans who support him directly.

"The money matters," he says. "But the relationship matters more. These people aren't just buying my music. They're investing in me. And I can see the impact."

He's started offering exclusive content to direct supporters — demos, behind-the-scenes videos, early access to new releases. He's building something that Bandcamp could never give him: ownership.

"I used to think I needed a platform to reach fans," he says. "Now I know I just need a way to accept payments. The rest is just music."

The bigger picture

Rafael's story is not unique. It's happening everywhere. Writers in Argentina selling essays directly to readers. Artists in Kenya selling prints without galleries. Developers in Vietnam selling tools without app stores. The infrastructure is simple, cheap, and under the creator's control.

BCH doesn't just save money. It saves autonomy. It gives creators a way to monetize without asking permission. It builds relationships, not transactions.

"I'm not trying to get rich," Rafael says. "I'm trying to make music. And now I can afford to do that. That's enough."
