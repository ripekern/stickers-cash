A freelancer paid in BCH

A freelancer in Argentina invoices a client in the US. The payment settles in minutes, costs pennies, and no one needs a bank account or approval.

Post 31 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

Maria is a graphic designer in Buenos Aires. She has a client in San Francisco who needs a new logo. They agree on $800. Maria sends an invoice, the client pays, and the money shows up in Maria's wallet before she finishes her coffee.

No bank transfer, no Western Union, no PayPal hold. Just a Bitcoin Cash payment that settled in seconds and cost less than a cent in network fees.

The old way

If Maria used a bank wire, she'd wait three to five business days and pay $25 to $45 in transfer fees. Her bank would convert the dollars to pesos at a rate that includes a hidden markup. She might lose 3% to 5% of the payment before it hits her account.

If she used PayPal, the client would pay a 2.9% fee plus $0.30, and Maria would pay another fee to withdraw to her bank. PayPal might also freeze the payment for review, delaying it by days or weeks. Maria has no recourse — PayPal's terms give them broad discretion to hold funds.

Both methods require Maria to have a bank account and to be "approved" by the payment provider. In Argentina, where banking access is limited and currency controls are strict, this is not just inconvenient — it's a barrier to working at all.

The BCH way

Maria sends her client a Bitcoin Cash address and the amount in BCH (calculated from the current exchange rate). The client opens their wallet, scans a QR code, and sends the payment. The transaction broadcasts to the network and confirms within minutes.

The fee is a fraction of a cent, regardless of the amount. There's no intermediary holding the funds, no conversion markup, no waiting for "business days." The money is Maria's as soon as the transaction confirms.

Maria can hold the BCH, convert it to pesos through a local exchange, or use it to buy things directly. She has options, not obligations.

Why this matters

For freelancers in countries with strong currencies and open banking, this might seem like a minor improvement. For freelancers in Argentina, Nigeria, Venezuela, or anywhere with currency controls or limited banking access, it's transformative.

BCH is not just a payment method — it's a way to participate in the global economy without asking permission. The freelancer and the client deal directly, peer to peer. The contract is between them, not between them and a bank.

The invoice flow

Here's how Maria structures her invoices:

  • Quote: Maria quotes $800 USD, with a note that payment is accepted in BCH at the exchange rate on the day of payment.
  • Invoice: When the client is ready to pay, Maria sends a Bitcoin Cash address and the exact BCH amount (calculated from a public price API). She includes a 15-minute window for the rate to stay valid.
  • Payment: The client scans a QR code or copies the address, sends the BCH, and emails a screenshot or transaction ID.
  • Confirmation: Maria checks her wallet, sees the payment, and sends a receipt. The job is done.

There's no payment processor, no merchant account, no chargeback risk. The payment is final once confirmed. If Maria wants to issue a refund, she sends a new payment — simple, transparent, and under her control.

Accounting and taxes

Maria keeps records in both USD and BCH. She logs the invoice amount in USD, the BCH amount received, the exchange rate used, and the date. For tax purposes, she reports the USD value of the income on the day she received it.

Some freelancers convert immediately to local currency to avoid holding BCH. Others keep a portion as a hedge against inflation. Both are deliberate choices, and both work. The important thing is that Maria decides, not a bank or a payment processor.

Privacy and security

Maria uses a new Bitcoin Cash address for each invoice. This makes it harder for anyone to link her payments together. Her wallet generates fresh addresses automatically, so she doesn't have to manage them manually.

She keeps her private keys secure, backed up on paper, and stored offline. Her wallet is watch-only on her phone — she can see incoming payments but can't spend from it. The spending keys are on a hardware wallet she only connects when she needs to move funds.

The freelancer's question

It's not "is this legal?" It's "can I get paid, and can I account for it?" BCH settles directly and cheaply, and the transaction is recorded on a public chain. That's a strong answer for a freelancer working across borders.

Building the flow

Maria doesn't need a website or a complex system. She uses a wallet app on her phone, a simple invoice template (she made it in Google Docs), and a public BCH/USD price API. When she's ready to invoice, she checks the rate, calculates the BCH amount, and sends the details.

For higher volume, she could set up a small website with an invoicing tool that auto-generates addresses and locks in rates. The stickers.cash site is a working example of this kind of setup. The Cloudflare Worker that powers it is open source and can be adapted for invoicing or freelance payments.

The point is that the infrastructure is simple, cheap, and under Maria's control. She doesn't need permission to work, and she doesn't need to pay a middleman to get paid.
