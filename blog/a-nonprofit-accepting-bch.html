A nonprofit accepting BCH

A small nonprofit accepts BCH donations. The money goes directly to their wallet, no platform fees, no chargebacks, and donors can verify where it went.

Post 32 of 37 · 4 min read · Oct 30, 2026

A wildlife rescue in Costa Rica needs to raise money for veterinary supplies. They put a Bitcoin Cash donation address on their website. Donors send BCH directly to the rescue's wallet. No platform takes a cut, no credit card company holds the funds, and anyone can verify the transactions on the blockchain.

This is not a hypothetical. It's how stickers.cash itself operates. The donation page is a single address, and the money goes straight to the shop's wallet. The model works for a sticker shop, and it works for a nonprofit.

The old way

Most nonprofits use PayPal, GoFundMe, or similar platforms. These services charge fees that add up: 2.9% plus $0.30 per donation, plus withdrawal fees, plus currency conversion markups for international donors. For a small donation of $20, the fees can eat 10% or more.

Worse, the platform holds the funds. If there's a dispute, a chargeback, or a policy violation, the nonprofit's money can be frozen. The nonprofit has limited recourse and no control over when they can access their own donations.

Some platforms also take a percentage of donations as a "platform fee." GoFundMe, for example, charges 2.9% plus $0.30 per donation in payment processing fees, on top of any optional tips donors add. For a $10 donation, the nonprofit might only receive $9.40.

The BCH way

The nonprofit publishes a Bitcoin Cash address on their website, social media, and fundraising materials. Donors send BCH directly to that address. The transaction settles in minutes, costs a fraction of a cent, and the money is in the nonprofit's wallet — no intermediary, no hold, no fee.

The nonprofit can hold the BCH, convert it to local currency when needed, or use it directly to pay for supplies from vendors who accept BCH. The choice is theirs, not a platform's.

Transparency and trust

Every BCH transaction is recorded on the blockchain. Donors can verify that their donation was received and see where the funds went. The nonprofit can publish transaction IDs to show how donations were spent — on veterinary supplies, on food, on shelter.

This is a level of transparency that traditional fundraising cannot match. A donor giving through PayPal has no way to verify that the money went where it was supposed to. With BCH, the ledger is public, and the nonprofit can prove it.

The donor's question

"Is my donation actually going to the cause?" With BCH, the donor can check the blockchain and see exactly where the money went. The nonprofit can publish receipts as transaction IDs. Trust is built on transparency, not on a platform's promises.

International donations

A donor in Japan wants to support a rescue in Kenya. With PayPal, the donor's currency is converted to USD, then to KES, with markups at each step. The rescue might receive 5% to 10% less than the donor intended.

With BCH, the donor sends BCH directly to the rescue's address. There's no currency conversion, no intermediary, and no markup. The rescue receives the full amount, minus a fraction of a cent in network fees.

For international giving, BCH is not just cheaper — it's faster. The donation settles in minutes, not days. The rescue can use the funds immediately, without waiting for a bank transfer to clear.

Building the donation flow

The nonprofit doesn't need a complex system. They need:

  • A BCH wallet: Selene, Electron Cash, or any wallet that supports BCH. The wallet holds the private keys and generates addresses.
  • A donation page: A simple HTML page with the donation address and a QR code. The stickers.cash donate page is a working example.
  • A way to track donations: The nonprofit can check their wallet to see incoming donations, or use a block explorer to verify transactions.

For higher volume, the nonprofit could set up a system that generates a fresh address for each donation, tracks the amounts, and sends receipts. The Cloudflare Worker that powers stickers.cash can be adapted for this purpose.

Accounting and compliance

The nonprofit keeps records of each donation: the date, the BCH amount, the USD value at the time of donation, and the donor's information (if provided). For tax purposes, the nonprofit reports the USD value of the donations received.

Some donors may want a receipt for tax deductions. The nonprofit can issue a receipt that includes the transaction ID, the BCH amount, and the USD value on the date of the donation. This is straightforward and compliant with most tax authorities.

Privacy for donors

Donors who want to remain anonymous can send BCH without revealing their identity. The transaction is public on the blockchain, but the donor's name is not attached to it. This is useful for donors who want to support a cause without public recognition.

Donors who want recognition can provide their name and contact information, and the nonprofit can acknowledge them publicly. The choice is the donor's, not the platform's.

Why this matters

For a small nonprofit, every dollar counts. Platform fees, conversion markups, and withdrawal fees add up. BCH removes those costs and gives the nonprofit direct control over their funds.

For donors, BCH offers transparency, speed, and lower costs. The donation goes directly to the cause, not to a middleman. The donor can verify the transaction and see where the money went.

For the global nonprofit sector, BCH is a way to accept donations from anywhere in the world, without the friction of traditional banking. A donor in one country can support a cause in another, quickly and cheaply.
