Scaling is a choice

Every payment network has a capacity limit. The question is what you do when you hit it: ration space, build layers, or make more space.

Post 19 of 20 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Every payment network has a capacity limit. A card network has servers and settlement windows. A blockchain has blocks. The interesting question is not whether a limit exists. It is what the network does when demand hits it.

Blocks are batches

A block is a batch of transactions. The block size limit decides how much can fit in one batch. If demand exceeds capacity, users bid for space through fees. If capacity exceeds demand, fees stay low. That is the basic economics of every block chain.

Bitcoin Cash made a deliberate choice: increase the batch size and keep payments cheap on the base layer. It launched in 2017 with 8 MB blocks, moved to 32 MB in 2018, and since 2024 has used an adaptive block-size limit that can grow with demand while keeping 32 MB as a floor. The goal is to make on-chain payments practical for ordinary amounts.

The trade-off is real

Bigger blocks are not free. They require more bandwidth to propagate, more storage to keep, and more processing power to validate. If the requirements grow too high, fewer people can run a full node, and the network becomes more dependent on large operators. That is the centralization concern, and it is legitimate.

Smaller blocks keep node requirements low but force more transactions into a fee market or onto secondary layers. That can make the base layer more expensive and push small payments elsewhere. It is a different set of trade-offs, not a free lunch.

Capacity is a spectrum

“Big blocks” and “small blocks” are shorthand for different bets about how people will use the chain. BCH bets that cheap on-chain payments are worth the higher resource cost. The adaptive limit is an attempt to let capacity follow demand instead of freezing the argument at one number forever.

The 2017 split was about more than a number

The Bitcoin Cash fork grew out of a disagreement about scaling, but the block size was only the visible part. The deeper disagreement was about what the network was for. One side wanted the base layer to be settlement for a layered system. The other wanted it to remain peer-to-peer cash that ordinary people could use directly.

BCH chose the second path. That choice shapes everything else: low fees, 0-conf acceptance for small purchases, and a strong preference for on-chain utility. It also means BCH inherits the burden of proving that bigger blocks can stay decentralized enough to be credible.

Scaling is not only technical

A network can have plenty of capacity and still fail to scale if wallets are confusing, merchants do not accept it, liquidity is thin, or regulation makes the on-ramps difficult. Technology sets the ceiling. Adoption decides whether you get near it.

BCH has added tools beyond simple payments, including CashTokens for assets and lightweight contracts on layer one. Those features expand what the chain can do, but they do not replace the basics: people need a reason to use it, a wallet they trust, and a place to spend it.

The honest summary is that BCH chose a direction and is still building toward it. The adaptive block limit answers the “what happens when demand grows?” question better than a fixed number, but it does not answer the “will people show up?” question. That one is settled in the world, not in the protocol.
