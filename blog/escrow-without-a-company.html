Escrow without a company

A 2-of-3 multisig or a small covenant can hold funds until the conditions are met. The middleman becomes a key, not a company.

Post 27 of 30 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Escrow is one of the oldest reasons to need a middleman. A buyer does not want to pay before delivery. A seller does not want to deliver before payment. A platform holds the money, takes a fee, and decides who is right when something goes wrong.

Bitcoin Cash moves the custody onto a contract. The payout rule is enforced by the network, and the judgment stays with people who are accountable. That removes the need for a company to hold the funds.

The 2-of-3 escrow

The classic design uses three keys: buyer, seller, and arbiter. The funds are locked in a 2-of-3 multisignature output. Any two keys can release the money. If the buyer and seller agree, they can settle without the arbiter. If they disagree, the arbiter sides with one of them. The arbiter cannot unilaterally take the money, because one key is not enough.

This is a real improvement over a platform escrow. The arbiter’s power is limited by the script. The funds are visible on-chain. The buyer and seller do not have to trust the arbiter’s balance sheet or wait for a withdrawal queue. The fee is a normal BCH transaction fee, not a percentage of the sale.

Adding time

Multisig handles cooperation. Timelocks handle disappearance. A contract can add a deadline: if the seller does not deliver and the buyer cannot get a response, the buyer can reclaim the funds after a certain block height. Or if the buyer goes silent after delivery, the seller can claim after a deadline.

Timelocks make the fallback automatic. If delivery does not happen by the deadline, the buyer can reclaim the funds. If the buyer goes silent after delivery, the seller can claim. The contract makes the default path clear and the exceptional path narrow, so both sides know the rules before they start.

Blind escrow

A covenant can go further than multisig. In a “blind escrow,” the contract itself restricts the possible recipients. The arbiter can approve a release, but the money can only go to the buyer or the seller. The arbiter cannot redirect it to a friend, a exchange, or themselves.

That is a meaningful check on the middleman. The arbiter helps resolve a disagreement, but the payout rule is enforced by the network. The contract can also require that the full amount be paid, or that a fee be deducted in a specific way, so the parties know the settlement math in advance.

Why it works

The chain handles custody and payout. Clear terms, evidence, and a reputable arbiter handle the human side. The difference is that the money is not sitting in the arbiter’s bank account while the dispute is discussed. It is locked in a contract both sides can inspect, and the payout can only go to the parties named in the rule.

For small transactions, a 2-of-3 setup is simple and effective. For larger ones, a covenant with restricted payouts and timelocks adds another layer of certainty. Either way, the custody rule is in code, and the judgment stays with people who are accountable.
