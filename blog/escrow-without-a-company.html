Escrow without a company

A shared-key setup or a small rule can hold funds until the conditions are met. The middleman becomes a key, not a company.

Post 28 of 37 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

Escrow is one of the oldest reasons to need a middleman. A buyer does not want to pay before delivery. A seller does not want to deliver before payment. A platform holds the money, takes a fee, and decides who is right when something goes wrong.

Bitcoin Cash moves the custody onto a contract. The payout rule is enforced by the network, and the judgment stays with people who are accountable. That removes the need for a company to hold the funds.

The shared-key setup

The classic design uses three keys: buyer, seller, and arbiter. The funds are locked so that any two of the three keys can release the money. If the buyer and seller agree, they can settle without the arbiter. If they disagree, the arbiter sides with one of them. The arbiter cannot take the money alone, because one key is not enough.

This is a real improvement over a platform escrow. The arbiter's power is limited by the rules. The funds are visible on the chain. The buyer and seller do not have to trust the arbiter's bank account or wait for a withdrawal. The fee is a normal BCH transaction fee, not a percentage of the sale.

Adding a deadline

Shared keys handle cooperation. Deadlines handle disappearance. A rule can add a time limit: if the seller does not deliver and the buyer cannot get a response, the buyer can reclaim the funds after a certain date. Or if the buyer goes silent after delivery, the seller can claim after a deadline.

Deadlines make the backup plan automatic. If delivery does not happen by the deadline, the buyer can reclaim the funds. If the buyer goes silent after delivery, the seller can claim. The rules make the normal path clear and the exception path narrow, so both sides know what happens before they start.

Restricting where the money goes

A more advanced rule can go further than shared keys. In a "restricted escrow," the rule itself limits who can receive the money. The arbiter can approve a release, but the money can only go to the buyer or the seller. The arbiter cannot redirect it to a friend, an exchange, or themselves.

That is a meaningful check on the middleman. The arbiter helps resolve a disagreement, but the payout rule is enforced by the network. The rule can also require that the full amount be paid, or that a fee be deducted in a specific way, so the parties know the math in advance.

Why it works

The chain handles custody and payout. Clear terms, evidence, and a reputable arbiter handle the human side. The difference is that the money is not sitting in the arbiter's bank account while the dispute is discussed. It is locked by rules both sides can inspect, and the payout can only go to the parties named in the rule.

For small transactions, a shared-key setup is simple and effective. For larger ones, restricted payouts and deadlines add another layer of certainty. Either way, the custody rule is in code, and the judgment stays with people who are accountable. The introspection opcodes that make covenants possible and cross-chain trades without an exchange show how the technical side works.

Escrow works on a single chain. But what if you need to trade coins across different chains — without trusting the other party?
