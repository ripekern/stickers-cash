Why waiting ten minutes is normal

Crypto payments feel instant because the message travels instantly. Settlement is a separate step — and ten minutes is the design, not a bug.

Post 13 of 20 · 3 min read · Oct 22, 2026

One of the most common complaints about crypto payments is that they are not really instant. The screen says “sent” immediately, but the payment is not final until it is in a block. For BCH, that block can take ten minutes on average. Is that slow?

It depends on what you mean by instant. The message travels in seconds. The settlement takes longer. Those are two different promises, and traditional payments blur them together.

Blocks are batches, not individual payments

BCH does not process one payment at a time. It collects valid transactions into a block and adds that block to the chain. The network targets one block every ten minutes. That target is an average, not a schedule. Because block discovery is probabilistic, you might get a block in one minute or wait forty. The average still comes out near ten.

A payment can be visible to the receiver as soon as it is broadcast. The merchant's wallet sees it in the mempool and can choose to accept it before confirmation. This is called a zero-confirmation payment, or 0-conf. It is a calculated risk: the sender could theoretically try to double-spend, but for small amounts the risk is usually not worth the friction of waiting.

Confirmations stack

Once a transaction is in a block, it has one confirmation. Each additional block makes it harder to reverse because an attacker would have to outrun the chain. Six confirmations is a common convention inherited from Bitcoin, but it is not a law. A four-dollar sticker does not need the same settlement assurance as a car.

For face-to-face purchases, many merchants accept 0-conf because the customer is standing there and the amount is small. For remote or high-value payments, waiting for one or more confirmations is the safer default. BCH also has tools such as double-spend proofs that help merchants detect certain conflicts, but no tool turns an unconfirmed transaction into a guaranteed final one.

Compare it with a card

A card payment feels instant because the authorization is instant. The money does not necessarily settle between banks for days. The merchant can also face a chargeback weeks or months later. The card network sits in the middle, which makes reversibility possible and also makes fraud, disputes, and fees possible.

BCH flips the sequence. The payment is final at the protocol level once confirmed. There is no chargeback department and no issuer to appeal to. That is good for a merchant who wants certainty, and bad for a customer who wants an undo button. The trade-off is real.

Why ten minutes?

Block time is a security parameter. Faster blocks mean more frequent chain tips, more propagation pressure, and more orphaned blocks. Slower blocks give the network time to agree. BCH could change the parameter, but it would not make settlement free. It would move the trade-off around.

The practical answer is to stop treating “sent” and “settled” as the same event. For a coffee, the visible broadcast is enough. For a laptop, wait for a confirmation. For a house, wait for more. The protocol gives you a spectrum; the amount at risk tells you where to stand on it.

The useful rule

Match the wait to the value. Small and local: 0-conf is often fine. Larger or remote: wait for confirmations. The goal is not to make every payment feel like a card. The goal is to know what you are trusting at each step.
